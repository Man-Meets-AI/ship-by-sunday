# Lesson 2.1 UX Critique: Find References First

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to move a paying student from a fuzzy idea to a shipped v1 over one guided weekend. The product works when each lesson removes a specific kind of ambiguity, produces a reusable artifact, and makes the next step feel more obvious than the last one.

### Surface goal

Lesson `2.1` needs to turn the CEO brief into a usable visual direction starting point. This surface should help the student collect references that are relevant to their chosen user, promised outcome, and constrained core flow, then carry that taste cleanly into sketching in `2.2`.

### Business model

This is a paid course product sold through Stripe and delivered in-app through Clerk and Convex. The business depends on fast activation after purchase, steady lesson progression across a weekend sprint, low confusion inside the product shell, and a strong sense that the system is improving the student's judgment, not just storing files.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.7` to `2.1` continuation rate
- `2.1` completion rate
- `2.1` to `2.2` continuation rate
- time from `2.1` load to first saved reference
- time from first saved reference to lesson completion
- downstream quality of `2.2` sketches and `2.4` rough screens
- support or refund signals around “what counts as a good reference?” or “why won’t this link save?”

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students enter `2.1` with a completed `product_brief` artifact because the lesson depends on it.
- Assumption: this is intentionally the first design lesson, so it should translate strategy into taste and reduce blank-page anxiety before the student opens Figma or sketches.
- Assumption: the critique is based on the coded lesson definition, shell behavior, reference-gallery field control, artifact context, and completion logic, not a live browser session or student recordings.
- Confidence: high on structural issues around flow, completion logic, and interaction design because those are explicit in code; medium on exact severity because this pass did not include live analytics or observed user sessions.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson rewards image count more than visual judgment.

User cannot tell what makes a reference useful, causing shallow completion, because the primary success condition is simply saving at least three images. The UI reinforces quantity through the checklist, progress rail, and completion logic, but it does not ask the student to explain what each reference is teaching them.

Impact:

- Students can complete the lesson with three attractive but strategically irrelevant screenshots.
- The product teaches collection behavior more than taste-building behavior.
- Downstream sketch quality in `2.2` is likely to vary widely because the artifact does not encode what to borrow.

### 2. The product brief is present, but not operationalized as a filter for what to collect.

User cannot confidently decide whether a reference belongs in the board, causing decision drift, because the pinned artifact context shows prior work but the core task copy does not actively translate that brief into a reference-collection rubric.

Current mismatch:

- The lesson says to collect what feels close to the desired style or flow.
- The resources panel points to broad inspiration sites.
- The product brief preview sits nearby, but the main surface never converts it into a simple “collect references for this user, outcome, and one core flow” rule.

Impact:

- Students can wander into generic inspiration browsing.
- The first design lesson risks breaking the tight scope discipline built in the CEO block.
- The system feels less like a guided method and more like a nice storage shell around external galleries.

### 3. The capture flow is more technical than the student’s mental model.

User cannot easily predict what inputs will work, causing friction and avoidable errors, because the field accepts uploads, pasted images, and direct image URLs, but explicitly rejects normal site links and requires a hidden “click zone to arm paste” behavior.

Examples:

- The helper copy says inspiration site links belong in Resources, but the user is likely to copy page URLs from those exact sites.
- The input asks for a “direct image URL,” which many non-technical users will not distinguish from a page URL.
- Paste readiness is signaled only after focusing the capture zone, which is a subtle interaction for a core input path.

Impact:

- The first Design lesson introduces mechanical friction right when it should feel easy and energizing.
- Students may think the product is broken when the URL they paste fails validation.
- A lesson meant to reduce blank-page anxiety can instead create tool anxiety.

### 4. The gallery behaves like a pile of images, not a working reference board.

User cannot easily turn collected references into reusable design direction, causing weak transfer into later lessons, because saved references have no lightweight structure beyond the image itself. There is no way to mark why an item matters, what pattern to borrow, or which references should lead the next sketch pass.

Impact:

- The board is useful for storage, but weak for synthesis.
- Students have to remember why they saved each image.
- The product misses a chance to help the student convert inspiration into concrete design constraints.

### 5. The split-shell hierarchy spreads attention across too many “primary” elements.

User cannot immediately understand the intended sequence, causing cognitive drag, because the lesson opens with status rail, artifact context, resources, and the gallery capture area all competing for attention. For a “first design step” lesson, the path should feel more linear than it currently does.

Impact:

- Students may start browsing resources before reading the brief context.
- Students may save images before understanding what qualities they are meant to borrow.
- The lesson feels more like a workspace to manage than a guided decision to make.

### 6. The lesson hands off too little to `2.2`.

User can finish `2.1` without forming a clear sketching direction, causing a weak next-step bridge, because completion requires only saved references, not a short articulation of what those references imply for the next sketch pass.

Impact:

- `2.2` has to recover synthesis that `2.1` could have created.
- Students may over-index on aesthetics instead of flow, hierarchy, and trust cues.
- The Design block risks feeling like separate activities rather than compounding decisions.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Add a lightweight “what am I borrowing?” layer to the reference board.

What to change:

- Require each saved reference to capture one quick reason, tag, or choice such as `flow`, `hierarchy`, `trust cue`, `layout`, or `tone`.
- If per-item metadata is too heavy, require a short summary below the gallery with prompts like:
  - `Borrow this flow pattern`
  - `Borrow this hierarchy rule`
  - `Avoid this aesthetic trap`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It changes the lesson from collection to interpretation.
- It helps the student build taste in a reusable way instead of just saving screenshots.

How it supports business growth:

- Better synthesis here should improve the quality of `2.2` and `2.4`, which raises perceived product value.
- It reinforces that the product teaches decision-making, not generic inspiration hunting.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Adding fields creates more effort in a lesson intended to stay light.
- The structure must stay lightweight or it will feel like homework instead of momentum.

How to validate:

- Review whether students can explain why each reference matters after a quick scan of their saved board.
- Compare downstream sketch quality and coherence before and after adding the interpretation layer.

### 2. Turn the pinned brief into an explicit collection rubric at the top of the lesson.

What to change:

- Add a compact “collect against this brief” strip above the gallery that restates:
  - who the product is for
  - the promised outcome
  - the one core flow to preserve
  - what not to broaden yet
- Rewrite the main action copy to say: “Collect references for this user, this outcome, and this constrained flow.”

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes relevance legible while the student browses or pastes references.
- It reduces the chance that external galleries pull the student into unrelated inspiration.

How it supports business growth:

- Tighter relevance should improve completion quality without increasing lesson length much.
- Better continuity from CEO to Design strengthens the feeling that the method is coherent and worth paying for.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too much brief detail could clutter the screen.
- The rubric needs to be short enough to stay usable during active collection.

How to validate:

- Ask QA users to decide whether a reference belongs after reading only the rubric.
- Review whether saved references become more flow-specific and product-relevant.

### 3. Make the input model match how students actually collect inspiration.

What to change:

- Support normal page URLs as saved source links even if they are not direct image URLs.
- Keep the current image-upload path, but present it as the preferred option and demote the technical “direct image URL” language.
- Replace the hidden paste mechanic with an always-visible dropzone instruction such as “Paste screenshot or drag image here.”

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces avoidable friction around common behaviors.
- It makes the lesson feel forgiving rather than technical.

How it supports business growth:

- Faster first success in the Design block improves activation and forward motion.
- Lower input friction reduces support burden and protects trust.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Supporting source links adds complexity to the artifact schema and rendering logic.
- Mixed item types need clear display rules so the board does not become messy.

How to validate:

- Track failed URL attempts and time to first successful saved reference.
- Run a short usability test where users gather references without any instruction from the facilitator.

### 4. Add curation states so the board becomes a decision tool, not a gallery dump.

What to change:

- Let students mark 1-2 references as `lead references` for the next sketch pass.
- Provide a simple sort or grouping view by borrowed pattern, not just a flat image grid.
- Add a “keep only what you want to borrow” cleanup pass before completion.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It converts the board into a usable direction set for `2.2`.
- It makes taste selection explicit instead of implied.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger handoff quality should improve student confidence and the perceived intelligence of the system.
- Better curation supports faster movement through the rest of the Design block.

Trade-offs or risks:

- More board controls can overcomplicate a short lesson.
- The interaction model should stay simple enough for mobile or quick laptop use.

How to validate:

- Check whether students reference their “lead references” during sketching.
- Review whether users delete low-signal images more often after curation affordances are added.

### 5. Make the lesson sequence more explicit and reduce split-attention on first load.

What to change:

- Reframe the screen into a visible 3-step flow:
  - `1. Read the brief filter`
  - `2. Browse sources or paste screenshots`
  - `3. Pick the references you want to carry into sketching`
- Collapse artifact context by default into a tighter summary if needed.
- Keep Resources available, but visually subordinate them to the act of saving and curating references.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the next action obvious.
- It reduces the “toolbench” feeling in a lesson that should feel like a guided decision.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger clarity at block entry supports activation and progression.
- The product feels more intentional and less like a flexible but self-serve workspace.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Reducing visible context can hide helpful information for some students.
- The new hierarchy needs testing on both desktop and narrower screens.

How to validate:

- Measure time to first saved reference and completion time after the hierarchy change.
- Ask QA users to narrate the first three things they think they should do on load.

### 6. Add a short exit bridge that carries reference decisions into `2.2`.

What to change:

- Before completion, ask for a one-line synthesis such as:
  - `In sketching, I want to preserve...`
  - `The main pattern I’m borrowing is...`
- Use that line in the completion banner or next-step footer for `2.2`.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It gives the lesson a real output beyond stored images.
- It helps the student start sketching with intent instead of starting over.

How it supports business growth:

- Better continuity across lessons improves weekend momentum and perceived product sharpness.
- A stronger bridge should reduce drop-off between collection and sketching.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Another required field adds friction if the prompt is too open-ended.
- The synthesis must stay concise or it will feel repetitive after the CEO block.

How to validate:

- Review whether students enter `2.2` with a clearer explanation of what they are trying to sketch.
- Compare `2.1` to `2.2` continuation and re-open patterns after the bridge is added.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

### Decision 1: optimize for judgment, not artifact volume

The current lesson optimizes for getting something saved quickly. That is useful, but this is the first Design lesson, so the product should optimize slightly more for relevance and interpretation than raw collection speed. The right move is not a heavy mood-board tool. It is a thin layer that makes students say what they are borrowing and why.

### Decision 2: reduce technical strictness where it does not teach the method

Rejecting page URLs and requiring direct image URLs creates precision that helps the data model more than the student. For this lesson, permissive capture and clear structure are more valuable than strict input purity.

### Decision 3: preserve speed, but add one synthesis moment before completion

This lesson should remain short and energizing. The solution is not a long note-taking step. The solution is one small bridge from “I saved references” to “I know what I am about to sketch.”

### Decision 4: keep the board simple, but make the brief impossible to ignore

The lesson already has the right dependency, `product_brief`. The missing move is not more prerequisite content. It is using that prerequisite as an active filter on the screen so the Design block feels like a continuation of the same method.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Add a visible brief-based collection rubric tied to the pinned `product_brief`.
- Reduce capture friction by supporting common reference-collection behaviors more gracefully.
- Add one synthesis requirement so completion means more than “three images saved.”

### Should

- Add lightweight reference labeling or tagging to encode what each image teaches.
- Introduce a simple “lead references” curation state for the next sketching step.
- Rework the first-load hierarchy so the sequence is clearer.

### Could

- Support grouped gallery views by borrowed pattern.
- Preserve source-site links alongside screenshots for later recall.
- Feed the `2.1` synthesis line directly into `2.2` as persistent context.

## Validation Plan

- Run browser QA on `2.1` with one user who has not seen the internal lesson design and observe whether they understand what counts as a good reference without coaching.
- Track time to first saved reference, failed URL attempts, and completion rate after any capture-flow changes.
- Review a sample of `2.1` artifacts and judge whether a reviewer could infer what the student plans to borrow from the saved board.
- Compare `2.1` to `2.2` continuation rate and the quality of `2.2` sketch artifacts after adding synthesis and curation.
- Add a quick internal rubric for artifact review:
  - relevance to chosen user and outcome
  - clarity of borrowed pattern
  - evidence of curation rather than dumping
  - readiness to sketch

## Narrative Summary

`2.1` has the right instinct: stop the student from designing from a blank brain. But the coded experience currently behaves more like a flexible image collector than a judgment-building design step. It asks the student to save references, yet it does not strongly teach how to choose them, why they matter, or how they should constrain the next lesson.

The best path is not to build a complex mood-board product. It is to tighten the teaching loop. Use the CEO brief as an active filter, make the capture model more forgiving, add one thin layer of interpretation, and end with a short synthesis that travels into sketching. That would keep the lesson fast while making it do more of the real work this first Design step is supposed to do.
