# Lesson 2.2 UX Critique: Sketch Before Software

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to get a paying student from fuzzy idea to shipped v1 over one guided weekend. The product wins when each lesson reduces one specific kind of ambiguity, creates evidence of progress, and makes the next lesson easier to start.

### Surface goal

Lesson `2.2` should turn the saved reference board from `2.1` into a rough screen direction the student can actually build from. This surface needs to push the learner off-screen fast, help them sketch the core flow without spiraling, and return them to the app with enough evidence and confidence to enter `2.3 Storyboard the Journey`.

### Business model

This is a paid course delivered in-app after Stripe checkout and authenticated through Clerk with Convex-backed course state. The business depends on fast activation, weekend momentum, low confusion between lessons, and a strong sense that the product is improving the student's product judgment rather than acting like a generic file cabinet.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `2.1` to `2.2` continuation rate
- `2.2` completion rate
- `2.2` to `2.3` continuation rate
- time from `2.2` load to first uploaded sketch
- time from first upload to lesson completion
- percentage of `2.3` students who appear to have enough material to storyboard cleanly
- support or refund signals around “what counts as enough sketches?” or “am I done if I uploaded one photo?”

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive with a completed `reference_board` artifact and can view it from the collapsed context panel.
- Assumption: the lesson is intentionally manual-first and should not become an in-app drawing tool.
- Assumption: the critique is based on the current lesson contract, route behavior, workbench implementation, and tests, not on live user-session recordings.
- Confidence: high on structural issues around the task model, completion logic, and lesson handoff because they are explicit in code; medium on exact severity because this pass did not include browser QA or live student analytics.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson completes on upload evidence, not on sketching quality or decision quality.

User cannot tell when they have sketched enough to move forward, causing shallow completion, because the coded win condition is simply one stored upload while the teaching copy says to sketch `3-5` rough screens. The system treats the stronger guidance as advice, not as the actual bar.

Impact:

- Students can upload one weak or partial photo and still feel “done.”
- The product risks teaching compliance with the upload mechanic more than confidence in sketching the flow.
- `2.3` may inherit low-quality inputs and have to recover basic structure that should have been established here.

### 2. The lesson hides the most important upstream context behind an optional disclosure.

User cannot easily sketch against the right constraints, causing drift, because `Product Brief` and `Reference Board` only appear under `Need context?` after the primary upload flow. That keeps the screen light, but it also makes the strategic anchors easy to ignore.

Impact:

- Students may sketch from memory instead of from the chosen user, outcome, and reference patterns.
- The Design block can feel disconnected from the CEO block and from `2.1`.
- The product gives up a chance to reinforce “sketch this one flow for this one user” at the moment it matters most.

### 3. The page tells students to sketch, but does not help them self-check the output before upload.

User cannot verify whether their sketch set is usable, causing uncertainty and weak confidence, because the “Sketch rules” card gives general guidance but no pre-upload checklist like “can someone else follow the flow?” or “is the outcome moment visible?” The completion system also does not encode any quality gate beyond file presence.

Impact:

- Students may upload blurry, fragmented, or over-detailed screens and still move on.
- Learners who are anxious about doing design “wrong” get little reassurance about what good enough looks like.
- Support burden can shift downstream to “do I need to redo this before storyboarding?”

### 4. The current hierarchy makes the lesson feel more like proof submission than thinking work.

User cannot immediately understand that sketching is the real job, causing the app to feel administrative, because the largest interaction on screen is the upload zone and the strongest success message becomes “saved” rather than “you now have a rough flow to storyboard.” The experience is efficient, but it frames the lesson around storage instead of decision-making.

Impact:

- The lesson loses some of the energy promised by “Sketch Before Software.”
- Students may hurry to finish the upload step without fully using the sketching exercise to resolve ambiguity.
- The product can feel like it values artifact capture more than better product thinking.

### 5. The bridge into `2.3` is too implicit.

User can finish `2.2` without understanding how these sketches should power the storyboard, causing a weak handoff, because the lesson copy says “move on” but does not explicitly translate the sketch set into the next decision: choosing the key frames and sequence for the core journey.

Impact:

- Students may enter `2.3` unsure which sketches matter most.
- The Design block risks reading as a series of uploads instead of a compounding design method.
- The value of `2.2` can feel lower than it is because its output is not narratively framed as the input to storyboarding.

### 6. Missing lesson video media hurts this lesson more than it hurts text-first lessons.

User loses confidence in the intended pace and tone, causing more hesitation, because the first card is “Watch first” and currently falls back to `Video not wired yet.` This lesson depends on momentum, permission, and demonstration, so a missing video leaves a more obvious gap than in lessons where the work is self-explanatory.

Impact:

- The screen opens with a broken-feeling promise.
- Students who want a quick demonstration of what “rough enough” means get none.
- The product feels less complete at a moment when it is trying to reduce fear around sketching.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Add a lightweight “good enough sketch set” quality gate before completion.

What to change:

- Add a short non-negotiable checklist near the upload area with rules like:
  - `Shows one core flow`
  - `Includes 3-5 rough screens or a clearly equivalent set`
  - `The outcome moment is visible`
  - `Another person could follow the sequence`
- This can stay as a compact manual checklist or a required confirmation step rather than becoming a complex scoring system.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It tells the student what “done” means in plain language.
- It keeps the lesson manual-first while raising the quality bar above “one file exists.”

How it supports business growth:

- Better sketch quality should improve `2.3` continuation and reduce downstream confusion.
- It reinforces that the product is coaching judgment, not just collecting artifacts.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Extra gating adds a little friction to a lesson designed for speed.
- If the checklist is too long, it will feel like homework and weaken momentum.

How to validate:

- Compare `2.2` completion quality before and after the checklist.
- Measure whether `2.3` starts faster and with fewer weak storyboard uploads.

### 2. Make the critical context visible without reopening the full side-rail density.

What to change:

- Pull one compact context strip above “Sketch rules” that summarizes:
  - chosen user
  - promised outcome
  - one or two repeated patterns from the reference board
- Keep the full `Need context?` disclosure for deeper review, but do not hide all upstream context by default.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It keeps the student sketching against the right constraints without forcing them to reopen prior lessons mentally.
- It preserves the focused feel while reducing drift.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger continuity across lessons increases perceived product coherence.
- Better constrained sketches should improve downstream artifact quality and trust in the method.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too much context could make the page feel busy again.
- The strip must be aggressively concise or it will compete with the main task.

How to validate:

- QA whether students can restate who they are sketching for and what flow they are sketching after a quick glance.
- Review whether uploaded sketches better reflect the chosen reference direction.

### 3. Reframe the upload card around what the upload proves, not just the storage action.

What to change:

- Change the dominant message from “Upload proof and move on” toward “Bring back your rough flow so we can use it in storyboarding.”
- Once at least one image is present, show a summary like “Sketch set captured. Next, turn these into a 5-7 frame journey.”

Why it solves the user problem:

- It keeps the student focused on the design outcome, not the file-management step.
- It makes the lesson feel like a real stage in the method rather than a checkpoint form.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger narrative framing improves perceived craft and instructional quality.
- It should increase confidence that each lesson compounds rather than resets.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Copy-only framing will not solve every quality issue by itself.
- The message still needs to stay blunt and fast to preserve pace.

How to validate:

- Ask QA users what they believe the purpose of the upload is after reading the card once.
- Watch whether more users can accurately predict what `2.3` will ask them to do next.

### 4. Add a pre-upload “sanity check” example instead of a full template-first detour.

What to change:

- Add one tiny visual or text example of what a valid rough sketch set looks like:
  - boxes and arrows are enough
  - labels can be ugly
  - sequence matters more than polish
- Keep the worksheet download secondary, but use the example to reduce fear for first-time sketchers.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It gives anxious learners permission to move fast without over-designing.
- It reduces the blank-page problem more effectively than generic encouragement alone.

How it supports business growth:

- Faster first success in Design protects activation and continuation.
- Students who feel guided are more likely to trust the product and finish the sprint.

Trade-offs or risks:

- A bad example could anchor students too narrowly.
- The example must show “rough enough,” not imply there is one correct layout.

How to validate:

- Test with new users who say they are “bad at design” and see whether they start sketching faster.
- Track whether uploads become less polished-but-unclear and more rough-but-usable.

### 5. Make the `2.3` handoff explicit at completion time.

What to change:

- After upload, show a direct transition note such as:
  - `In the next lesson, pick the key frames from these sketches and order them into one clear journey.`
- If multiple sketches exist, suggest choosing the strongest path rather than carrying every variation forward.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It translates the current artifact into the next action.
- It reduces the cognitive reset between lessons.

How it supports business growth:

- Better handoffs increase flow-through completion across the Design block.
- The product feels more like a guided system, which improves perceived value and retention.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too much next-step copy can make the current lesson feel less complete.
- The handoff should reinforce momentum, not sound like another assignment.

How to validate:

- Measure `2.2` to `2.3` continuation and time-to-first-action in `2.3`.
- Ask QA users to explain what they think `2.3` is for before they open it.

### 6. Treat missing video as a launch-quality issue, not a neutral placeholder.

What to change:

- Replace `Video not wired yet.` with a more purposeful fallback until media is attached, for example:
  - one short “how to use this lesson” note
  - one screenshot or diagram of rough-enough sketches
  - a direct statement that no polished mockup is expected
- Still prioritize attaching the real lesson media because this lesson benefits heavily from demo energy.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces the feeling that the first card is broken.
- It preserves momentum even before the full media pipeline is live.

How it supports business growth:

- Fewer broken-feeling surfaces protect trust during activation.
- A better fallback reduces the risk that launch testers judge the lesson as incomplete.

Trade-offs or risks:

- A fallback can become permanent if the team stops treating video as required.
- It should not become a dense text block that defeats the purpose of the missing demo.

How to validate:

- Browser-QA the lesson with and without real media.
- Track whether users stall longer on lessons that still show placeholder video states.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

The strongest decision here is to keep `2.2` manual-first and upload-first, but stop pretending that file presence alone is enough evidence of learning. The right trade-off is not to add an in-app canvas or a heavy worksheet workflow. It is to preserve the fast off-screen sketch behavior while adding just enough context, quality guidance, and handoff framing that the artifact becomes useful, not merely present.

If scope is tight, I would not invest first in richer gallery management or per-image metadata. The better near-term move is:

- show the right context by default
- define what “good enough” means
- frame the upload as a bridge to storyboarding
- improve the placeholder state while video is still missing

That combination gives the highest user-value lift without turning a 15-minute sprint task into a slower design system exercise.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Add a compact quality gate so completion requires more than raw file existence.
- Expose a concise upstream context strip by default.
- Rewrite the upload and saved-state copy so the lesson clearly prepares `2.3`.
- Replace the empty-feeling video placeholder with a stronger temporary fallback until media is attached.

### Should

- Add one tiny “rough enough” example to reduce sketch anxiety.
- Tighten post-upload messaging so learners know whether one image is enough and what to do if they have multiple variants.

### Could

- Add lightweight curation later, such as marking the sketch or path to carry into storyboarding.
- Add analytics around time-to-upload and multi-image behavior once launch instrumentation is ready.

## Validation Plan

1. Browser-QA lesson `2.2` on desktop and mobile with the current auth flow and confirm the page still feels fast after adding the extra guidance.
2. Test three scenarios:
   - first-time learner with one sketch photo
   - learner with multiple sketches and variants
   - anxious learner who does not know what “good enough” means
3. Measure whether QA users can answer these questions without prompting:
   - Who am I sketching for?
   - What counts as enough for this lesson?
   - What am I doing in `2.3` with these sketches?
4. Compare downstream `2.3` artifacts before and after the changes to see whether storyboard quality improves.
5. Once analytics exists, track:
   - load to first upload
   - upload to completion
   - `2.2` to `2.3` continuation
   - number of support issues tied to sketch uncertainty or missing guidance

## Narrative Summary

`2.2` is close to the right product move. It correctly resists the temptation to build a fake in-app design tool and instead tells the student to think on paper first. That is the right backbone. The weakness is that the current experience makes the upload mechanic feel more real than the design decision it is supposed to capture.

The lesson should still be fast, blunt, and manual. But it needs a clearer definition of “good enough,” a visible link back to the brief and references, and a more explicit handoff into storyboarding. Right now it captures proof. With a few targeted UX changes, it can capture proof of the right thinking.
