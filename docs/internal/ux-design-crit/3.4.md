# Lesson 3.4 UX Critique: Wire DB, Auth, and Payments

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to make the first real integration pass feel controlled enough that a student can keep building instead of spiraling. In the Build block, the product promise is not just “learn the stack,” but “use the stack in a reliable sequence that gets you to a working v1 faster.”

### Surface goal

Lesson `3.4` should help the student connect database, auth, and payments into one reproducible local setup. When they leave, they should know the provider wiring was done in the right order, know what proof confirms each step, and have a saved record they can use when they boot local test mode in `3.5`.

### Business model

This is a premium guided execution product. A provider-wiring lesson has to reduce integration risk, because this is where the student’s belief in the product can break quickly. If the app allows students to mark a complex setup step complete with weak evidence, the product feels less like a guided system and more like a motivational checklist.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `3.3` to `3.4` continuation rate
- `3.4` completion rate
- percentage of `3.5` starts that do not require re-opening `3.4`
- support volume around Clerk/Convex issuer mismatches, missing env keys, and Stripe webhook confusion
- first-pass success rate for local test-mode validation after `3.4`
- drop-off rate between `3.4` and `3.5`

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: the student finished `3.3` and has enough tool access to follow provider documentation, but still needs the lesson to reduce sequencing mistakes.
- Assumption: the highest UX risk in `3.4` is false-positive completion, not lack of information.
- Assumption: the lesson needs to support bouncing between code, dashboards, and local notes without losing state.
- Confidence: high on the structural issues because they are visible in the lesson definition, shared shell behavior, and completion logic. Medium on exact severity because this pass was repo-based rather than authenticated browser QA.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson can be completed without proving that the integration stack actually works.

The required checklist has four items, but completion logic only checks whether each checkbox is checked. The required verification log only needs non-empty text. A student can type vague notes and check all four items without proving env keys are present, Clerk and Convex are aligned, Stripe objects exist, or the local flow passes.

Impact:

- Students can unlock `3.5` with an integration stack that is not actually ready.
- The product records confidence instead of reality at the point where reality matters most.
- Support burden gets deferred into the next lesson, where the student expects testing to work.

### 2. The lesson compresses multiple different jobs into one flat checklist.

`3.4` asks the student to do environment setup, auth configuration, Stripe dashboard setup, webhook wiring, and local validation. Those are different task types across different tools, but the workbench presents them as one flat sequence with no dependency guardrails beyond text copy.

Impact:

- Students can jump ahead to Stripe or local validation before auth and env wiring are stable.
- The lesson does not help users manage context switching between code, Clerk, Convex, and Stripe.
- A high-risk integration pass feels deceptively simple on the page and overly messy in practice.

### 3. The verification log is too unstructured for such a fragile setup step.

The placeholder suggests useful proof, but the field is still one textarea. That means env keys, JWT template evidence, Stripe object IDs, webhook path, and local-test result all compete inside one block of text. The artifact may be technically complete but operationally hard to scan and hard to reuse.

Impact:

- Students may omit one critical detail while still satisfying the form.
- Saved artifacts become weak handoffs into `3.5` and later debugging.
- The course loses a chance to turn fragile setup work into durable recovery context.

### 4. The lesson does not turn prior artifacts into active guidance.

`tooling_stack_ready` and `agent_build_brief` are pinned, but the workbench mostly treats them as side context. It does not say how the earlier tool setup should now be used, which provider comes first in this repo, or what the student should inspect in code before jumping into dashboards.

Impact:

- `3.3` and `3.4` feel adjacent rather than cumulative.
- Students have to infer the actual working order from external docs and prior memory.
- The guided system feels weaker exactly when sequencing matters most.

### 5. The external-workspace UI is too generic for a lesson with real failure modes.

The `External workspace` card tells the student to open the code workspace and keep the brief beside it. That is too generic for a lesson where specific failure points are known: env drift, Clerk issuer mismatch, missing JWT template naming, Stripe webhook miswiring, and inconsistent local host usage.

Impact:

- The lesson acknowledges complexity in copy, but does not operationalize it in the UI.
- Students do not get enough in-product steering for the most common places they can go wrong.
- The surface feels less productized than the underlying problem deserves.

### 6. The handoff to `3.5` is not explicit enough.

`3.5` depends on `integration_setup_complete`, yet `3.4` does not clearly define what the student should now be able to do before moving on. The lesson says “connected local stack with verification notes,” but it does not convert that into a crisp readiness statement for sandbox mode and local boot.

Impact:

- The unlock feels formal instead of behaviorally meaningful.
- Students may enter `3.5` unsure whether they are supposed to keep wiring or start testing.
- The Build arc loses momentum at a critical transition.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Replace the single checklist-plus-log model with proof-based integration checkpoints.

What to change:

- Turn each major checkpoint into a structured capture block:
  - env keys configured
  - Clerk + Convex auth aligned
  - Stripe product/price/webhook created
  - local validation run
- For each block, require:
  - done state
  - short proof note
  - one specific artifact such as command, path, object ID, template name, or test result

Why it solves the user problem:

- It raises the bar from “I think I did it” to “I can point to what proves it.”
- Students can debug faster because the product remembers the right details.

How it supports business growth:

- Fewer false completions should improve `3.5` success and reduce support noise.
- The product feels more like a real guided operator console.

Trade-offs or risks:

- The lesson gets more explicit and slightly heavier.
- The capture model must stay concise enough to preserve Build momentum.

How to validate:

- Review saved `integration_setup_complete` artifacts for concrete, reusable evidence.
- Compare `3.5` blocker rates before and after proof-based checkpoints.

### 2. Group the lesson by context, not just by task list.

What to change:

- Break the workbench into sections such as:
  - `Code + env`
  - `Auth alignment`
  - `Stripe setup`
  - `Local verification`
- Show the dependency order visually so students do not treat all steps as parallel.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It helps students manage tool switching and understand what has to be stable before the next move.
- The sequence becomes easier to follow under cognitive load.

How it supports business growth:

- Better sequencing should reduce abandonment in the hardest part of the Build flow so far.
- The course feels more intentionally choreographed and therefore more premium.

Trade-offs or risks:

- More sectioning adds UI complexity.
- The product has to keep the page skimmable, not turn it into a long setup manual.

How to validate:

- Browser-QA whether students can explain the sequence and current state without additional prompting.
- Measure whether users spend less time bouncing back to earlier steps after re-opening the lesson.

### 3. Turn the verification log into a structured handoff artifact for `3.5`.

What to change:

- Replace one large verification textarea with a small set of targeted fields:
  - env status
  - Clerk template evidence
  - Stripe object/webhook evidence
  - local validation result
  - unresolved issue note
- Preserve one optional freeform note for edge cases only.

Why it solves the user problem:

- Students can scan their own setup quickly when they return.
- Later lessons inherit a usable operational record rather than a wall of text.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger artifacts reduce repeated setup confusion and make support faster.
- The product earns trust by holding useful context, not just completion state.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Structured fields require more artifact design up front.
- If over-specified, the form could feel bureaucratic.

How to validate:

- Re-open a saved `3.4` artifact and check whether a student can prepare for `3.5` in under a minute.
- Review whether support/debugging conversations need less context reconstruction.

### 4. Pull known failure modes into the workbench as task guidance, not just external docs.

What to change:

- Add inline “watch for this” notes tied to the right step:
  - `convex` JWT template name must be exact
  - issuer/domain alignment matters
  - webhook secret parity matters across app and Convex
  - `localhost` consistency matters during local buyer-flow testing
- Keep the official docs, but pair them with the local failure bar.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces the need for students to discover common pitfalls the hard way.
- It makes the lesson feel actively protective, not passively informative.

How it supports business growth:

- Fewer avoidable integration mistakes means less drop-off and fewer trust hits.
- The product differentiates itself by teaching where people actually fail.

Trade-offs or risks:

- The warning set needs maintenance as implementation changes.
- Too many warnings could feel intimidating if not scoped tightly.

How to validate:

- Browser-QA fresh setups and check whether users still hit the same known mistakes.
- Track support messages for a drop in predictable configuration failures.

### 5. Use prior lesson context to make the task feel specific to this repo and slice.

What to change:

- Add a short bridge card that uses `tooling_stack_ready` and the Build brief to explain:
  - what codebase the student is wiring
  - why Playwright and provider skills matter now
  - what this setup unlocks in `3.5`
- Anchor the lesson in the student’s actual repo journey rather than generic integration guidance.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces context drift between setup and execution.
- Students understand why this step exists in the sequence, not just what to click.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger continuity improves perceived coherence across the Build block.
- Students are more likely to feel that progress comes from the system, not from improvising around it.

Trade-offs or risks:

- The bridge copy has to stay brief to avoid adding more reading overhead.
- Highly custom repos still require suggestion-style language rather than over-precise instructions.

How to validate:

- QA whether students can describe how `3.4` builds on `3.3` after one read.
- Review whether saved notes reference the actual repo and stack more often.

### 6. Make the `3.4 -> 3.5` readiness state explicit.

What to change:

- Add a completion checkpoint that spells out what the student should now be able to do:
  - switch to sandbox safely
  - regenerate webhook values
  - boot local without first discovering missing provider config
- Tie the footer copy and celebration state to that readiness, not just to artifact completion.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It clarifies whether the student is done wiring and ready to start environment testing.
- The unlock becomes meaningful, not ceremonial.

How it supports business growth:

- Better transition framing should improve continuation into `3.5`.
- The system feels more dependable when each lesson hands off a clear next-state.

Trade-offs or risks:

- The footer becomes slightly more instructional.
- The readiness claim must stay honest and not overpromise actual runtime success.

How to validate:

- Browser-QA the `3.4 -> 3.5` transition with fresh and partially-configured states.
- Track whether fewer users reopen `3.4` immediately after starting `3.5`.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

- I would optimize `3.4` for truthful setup state over short-form simplicity. This is a bad place for “lightweight” if lightweight means unverifiable.
- I would keep official provider docs as support material, but not rely on them to define success. Success needs to be legible in-product.
- I would not merge this lesson into `3.5`. Wiring and test-mode boot are different cognitive jobs, and separating them is correct. The issue is the weakness of the handoff, not the lesson boundary itself.
- I would favor structured evidence over richer UI polish. The biggest risk here is hidden integration debt, not a lack of visual refinement.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Replace trust-based checklist completion with structured proof per integration checkpoint.
- Restructure the surface into ordered sections that match the actual provider-wiring workflow.
- Make `integration_setup_complete` a reusable artifact for `3.5`, not just a completion token.

### Should

- Pull known failure modes into the in-product task flow.
- Add a bridge from `3.3` tooling setup and the Build brief into this lesson’s provider sequence.
- Make the `3.4 -> 3.5` readiness state explicit in footer and completion copy.

### Could

- Add short example verification entries so students know what “good evidence” looks like.
- Add a collapsible “common integration failures” panel if support issues stay high.
- Add structured timestamps or last-verified markers if later QA workflows need them.

## Validation Plan

- Run authenticated browser QA on `3.4` with a fresh local setup and a partially-configured setup.
- Confirm the lesson cannot be completed without concrete evidence for env, auth, Stripe, and local validation steps.
- Review saved `integration_setup_complete` artifacts for scanability and usefulness in `3.5`.
- Track support issues related to issuer mismatch, webhook drift, and missing env keys before and after the redesign.
- Measure `3.4` to `3.5` continuation and first-pass success when switching to sandbox and booting local.

## Narrative Summary

Lesson `3.4` is one of the most trust-sensitive steps in the whole Build block. It asks the student to connect auth, data, and payments, yet the current UX still behaves like a generic checklist: four checkboxes, one large verification note, and a polite assumption that the work really happened. That is too weak for the kind of setup debt this lesson can create.

The better version of `3.4` is still compact, but much more truthful. It should guide students through provider wiring in the order that actually reduces failure, require a small amount of concrete proof per checkpoint, and save that proof in a form the next lesson can actually use. If that happens, `3.4` stops being a ceremonial integration checkpoint and becomes what the Build sequence needs: the moment where the stack becomes real enough to test.
