# Lesson 1.7 UX Critique: CEO Block Wrap-Up

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to move a paying student from an unclear idea to a shipped v1 over one guided weekend. The product works when each lesson converts prior thinking into a clearer decision, a reusable artifact, and a credible next step toward shipping.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.7` needs to consolidate the CEO block into a design-ready handoff. This surface should help the student confirm that their user, outcome, evidence, flow, and scope now form one coherent product brief, then create a clean psychological and operational handoff into `2.1`.

### Business model

This is a paid course product sold via Stripe and delivered in-app through Clerk and Convex. The business depends on fast activation, sustained lesson progression, low confusion during the weekend sprint, and a strong feeling that the system is carrying the student forward with increasing clarity.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.6` to `1.7` continuation rate
- `1.7` completion rate
- `1.7` to `2.1` continuation rate
- time from `1.7` load to completion
- reopen rate from `2.1` back into CEO lessons
- support or refund signals around “what is my brief?” or “what should Design do next?”
- downstream quality of `2.1` references and `2.4` rough-screen decisions

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive here with all CEO artifacts technically present because `1.7` depends on them.
- Assumption: this lesson is meant to be more than a ceremony; it is supposed to create confidence that Design now has real inputs.
- Assumption: the critique is based on the coded lesson contract, stage-unlock summary builder, shell completion logic, and wrap-up renderer, not a live browser session or recorded student behavior.
- Confidence: high on the structural issues because the completion contract and stage-unlock renderer are explicit in code; medium on exact severity because this pass did not include live analytics or browser observation.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The screen claims the unlock before the student performs the final confirmation.

User cannot feel that they earned the transition into Design, causing the milestone to feel flatter and slightly less trustworthy, because the hero headline says `Design unlocked` on initial render while the actual completion action still sits below as `Unlock Design`.

Impact:

- The page weakens its own payoff structure.
- The lesson reads like the state is already complete before the final action happens.
- The product loses a moment that should reinforce progress and momentum going into the next block.

### 2. The lesson is effectively auto-complete as soon as the student lands.

User is not asked to make a meaningful final judgment, causing the wrap-up to feel ceremonial instead of useful, because the objective tasks for `1.7` are derived from upstream artifact presence and assembled summary sections, not from a distinct review or sign-off action on this page.

Impact:

- Students can click the CTA immediately without actually checking whether the brief tells a coherent story.
- The completion rail measures artifact existence, not handoff readiness.
- The product misses a chance to strengthen confidence right before the Design block starts.

### 3. The “brief” is assembled as a recap, but not yet framed as a design tool.

User cannot immediately tell what Design should now preserve, explore, or refuse to expand, causing a weaker handoff, because the stage-unlock summary mostly restates prior lesson outputs instead of translating them into design-relevant constraints.

Examples in the current surface:

- `Flow` resolves to `1 core flow photo saved for Design`
- `Evidence` can collapse into a signal count plus one summary line
- `Scope` emphasizes counts and rationale, but not explicit design guardrails

Impact:

- The artifact feels like a retrospective summary more than a brief someone could design from.
- Students may enter `2.1` treating the CEO block as “done” rather than “active input.”
- The Design block has to recover intent that should have been made explicit here.

### 4. The highest-visibility stats reward completeness signals more than decision quality.

User sees reassuring counts, but not whether the handoff is sharp enough, causing false confidence, because the top stat row highlights things like `1 user locked`, `8 research signals`, and `4 cuts made` rather than whether the brief is specific, differentiated, and constrained.

Impact:

- The summary looks finished even if the underlying choices are still weak or generic.
- Count-based reinforcement can validate shallow work.
- The product teaches that “captured enough items” is the success condition rather than “created a designable brief.”

### 5. The surface lacks direct recovery paths when the assembled brief feels weak.

User who notices a problem cannot repair it from within the wrap-up, causing friction and avoidance, because the page does not offer targeted “go fix this” actions back to the user, outcome, flow, or scope lessons even though those are the exact sources of truth.

Impact:

- Students must remember which prior lesson controls which piece of the brief.
- Weak students are more likely to continue with a shaky handoff rather than backtrack intentionally.
- The system feels less guided precisely when it should help the user decide whether to proceed or repair.

### 6. The transition into `2.1` is emotionally clear, but operationally under-instructed.

User knows the next lesson is `Find References First`, but not exactly how to use the newly assembled brief inside that lesson, causing a decision gap, because the page celebrates the unlock without giving a short operating rule like “collect references for this user, this promised outcome, and this one core flow only.”

Impact:

- The student may interpret the Design block too broadly.
- The hard-won scope discipline from `1.6` can soften immediately.
- The first design lesson has to restate context the wrap-up could have carried forward more directly.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Make the hero state truthful until the student completes the lesson.

What to change:

- Before completion, use a headline such as `CEO brief ready to lock` or `Review the CEO brief`.
- After completion, switch the celebration state to `Design unlocked`.
- Keep the current celebratory copy, but reserve it for the completion banner and post-click state.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It restores cause and effect.
- The student can feel the difference between review mode and unlocked mode.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger milestone design improves perceived polish and momentum.
- A more satisfying block transition supports progression into the Design lessons.

Trade-offs or risks:

- The pre-completion hero will feel slightly less exciting.
- The copy transition needs to be implemented carefully so it does not feel like duplicate messaging.

How to validate:

- Run a short QA test and ask whether the unlock feels earned or premature.
- Compare `1.7` completion rate and click latency before and after the copy-state split.

### 2. Add one explicit review-and-lock step that measures handoff readiness, not just artifact existence.

What to change:

- Add a short confirmation layer above the CTA with 2-3 checks such as:
  - `A designer could tell who this is for`
  - `The promised outcome is specific`
  - `The v1 is still brutally small`
- Require those checks, or a single `I reviewed the brief` action with sharper copy, before the completion CTA activates.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It forces a final pass over the assembled brief instead of allowing instant completion.
- It turns the wrap-up into a real judgment point.

How it supports business growth:

- Better handoff quality should improve downstream design quality.
- A stronger review step reduces false progress and preserves trust in the course sequence.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Any extra step adds friction to what is intentionally a short lesson.
- The confirmation must stay lightweight so the wrap-up does not become bureaucratic.

How to validate:

- Review whether students spend slightly more time on `1.7` while still continuing into `2.1`.
- In QA, ask students to summarize the brief before clicking the unlock CTA.

### 3. Reframe the assembled sections around design inputs, not recap categories.

What to change:

- Keep the existing five-part structure, but rewrite each section so it reads like a handoff:
  - `Who we are designing for`
  - `What outcome the screen must deliver`
  - `What evidence should shape design choices`
  - `What core flow Design must preserve`
  - `What not to design yet`
- For `Flow`, show a one-line sequence next to the saved image if possible, not only `1 core flow photo saved for Design`.
- For `Scope`, make the “not yet” boundary explicit, not just counts plus rationale.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It translates the CEO block into design language.
- It gives the next block a clearer operational contract.

How it supports business growth:

- Better handoff quality should increase continuity across blocks and reduce rework.
- Students are more likely to feel the course is compounding rather than repeating itself.

Trade-offs or risks:

- More explicit framing may duplicate some information already captured upstream.
- The brief must stay concise enough to feel usable, not like a long planning document.

How to validate:

- Review whether `2.1` references become more tightly aligned to the chosen user, outcome, and scope.
- Ask QA users what Design should do next after a 20-second scan of the wrap-up page.

### 4. Replace some top-row counts with “quality-of-handoff” signals.

What to change:

- Keep one or two counts if they help orientation, but change the row to emphasize readiness, for example:
  - `User clarity: locked`
  - `Outcome: specific`
  - `Evidence: grounded`
  - `Flow: visible`
  - `Scope: constrained`
- If counts remain, subordinate them inside sections instead of giving them prime emphasis.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It helps the student evaluate whether the handoff is usable, not just populated.
- It reduces the chance that quantity feels like quality.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger quality cues reinforce the core promise that the system is teaching judgment, not just data entry.
- That should improve retention and perceived product sharpness.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Quality labels require clearer logic and may involve more subjective thresholds.
- Overstating readiness can backfire if the underlying artifacts are still weak.

How to validate:

- Compare student explanations of the brief before and after the stat-row change.
- Watch whether support questions about “what makes this ready?” decrease.

### 5. Add targeted repair links from each section back to the source lesson.

What to change:

- Add small actions on each section card such as `Edit user`, `Edit outcome`, `Edit flow`, `Edit scope`.
- These should deep-link back to the relevant lesson route rather than forcing the student to navigate manually.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes repair fast and safe.
- It helps students correct the weak part without losing the overall narrative.

How it supports business growth:

- Better correction loops reduce abandonment and false completion.
- The product feels more intentionally guided, which supports trust.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Additional links can visually clutter the stage-unlock layout.
- Some users may over-edit if the repair paths are too prominent.

How to validate:

- Track reopen behavior from `1.7` into earlier CEO lessons.
- In QA, see whether users can identify and fix a weak section without confusion.

### 6. Add one explicit “how to use this in Design” bridge above the CTA.

What to change:

- Add a short transition card near the completion area:
  - `In Design, collect references for this user, this promised outcome, and this one core flow only. Do not widen the product again.`
- Optionally show the three most important carry-forward constraints in one compact strip:
  - `User`
  - `Outcome`
  - `Scope boundary`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It converts celebration into instruction.
- It makes the next block feel connected rather than just unlocked.

How it supports business growth:

- Better continuity into `2.1` should improve the quality of the first design decisions.
- It preserves the weekend-shippable promise by keeping scope discipline active across the transition.

Trade-offs or risks:

- This adds copy to a lesson that is intentionally short.
- The bridge must stay operational and tight, not motivational.

How to validate:

- Review whether students in `2.1` select references closer to the actual product brief.
- In QA, ask what they should ignore during the Design block after reading `1.7`.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

### Decision 1: Preserve the short celebratory nature of the lesson, but make it do one real job.

The right move is not to turn `1.7` into another long form lesson. The better trade-off is a short review-and-lock experience that preserves momentum while making the handoff more intentional.

### Decision 2: Optimize for handoff clarity over spectacle.

The current stage-unlock treatment looks like a milestone screen, which is directionally right. The next improvement should prioritize the usefulness of the brief and the transition into `2.1`, even if that means slightly less decorative emphasis on stats and celebration.

### Decision 3: Let the page support repair without encouraging endless rework.

Direct repair links are worth adding, but they should be small and clearly secondary. The page should make correction possible without inviting another spiral of reconsideration.

### Decision 4: Keep the composed brief, but translate it into downstream language.

Auto-composing the brief from pinned artifacts is efficient and consistent with the architecture. The issue is not that the artifact is assembled automatically; the issue is that the current assembly reads more like a summary than a design handoff. The recommendation is to improve the framing, not to abandon the composed-brief model.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Make the hero and completion states truthful so the unlock happens after action, not before it.
- Add one lightweight review-and-lock confirmation before completion.
- Reframe the brief sections so they read like design inputs, especially `Flow` and `Scope`.
- Add one explicit bridge that tells the student how to use the brief in `2.1`.

### Should

- Replace or soften the count-heavy top stat row with handoff-readiness cues.
- Add targeted repair links back to the source lessons for weak sections.

### Could

- Personalize the completion banner with a shorter brief summary, not just the next lesson label.
- Show a compact one-line flow sequence alongside the saved flow image if that data becomes available upstream.

## Validation Plan

- Run a focused browser QA pass on `1.7` with one complete CEO artifact set and observe whether the student can explain the brief before clicking the CTA.
- Ask three questions in QA:
  - `Who is this for?`
  - `What outcome are we designing toward?`
  - `What should Design refuse to expand yet?`
- Measure `1.7` to `2.1` continuation rate after the critique-informed changes.
- Review early `2.1` reference galleries and check whether the references clearly reflect the user, outcome, and scope from the CEO brief.
- Track whether students use repair links and whether those corrections reduce later confusion instead of increasing churn.

## Narrative Summary

`1.7` is trying to be the “you earned it” moment at the end of the CEO block, and the stage-unlock direction is mostly right. The current problem is that the lesson celebrates the unlock before the student actually locks anything, and it measures assembled artifacts more than handoff quality. That makes the page feel polished, but slightly hollow.

The strongest version of this lesson is a short, confident review surface that helps the student verify one thing: Design now has a real brief to work from. If the page tells the truth about the milestone, asks for one explicit sign-off, translates the recap into design inputs, and bridges directly into `2.1`, then the CEO block will end with both momentum and clarity. That is the point of the lesson, and it is also the point where the product proves that earlier work was not busywork.
