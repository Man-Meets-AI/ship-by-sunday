# Lesson 1.2 UX Critique: Pick One User

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to get a paying student from a vague product instinct to a scoped, buildable v1 in one guided weekend sprint. The course only works if each lesson produces a durable artifact that sharpens the next decision instead of creating worksheet churn.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.2` has to force the student to commit to one user by choosing one candidate problem from `1.1`, then grounding that choice in a specific pain moment, current workaround, and near-term path to reach that user. The lesson should turn "one of these sounds good" into "this is the exact person and situation I am building for."

### Business model

This is a paid course product sold through Stripe with access handled through Clerk and Convex. Revenue quality depends on buyer activation, continued progress through the sprint, perceived instructional quality, and lower refund/support risk from people who feel lost after purchase.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.1` to `1.2` progression rate
- `1.2` completion rate
- `1.2` to `1.3` continuation rate
- Time to first saved draft in `1.2`
- Failed completion attempts on `1.2`
- Downstream quality of `1.3` outcome statements and `1.4` research inputs
- Support signals around "who am I building for?" or "how specific should this be?"

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive here with three candidate problems from `1.1`, but those candidates vary widely in quality.
- Assumption: the student is likely capable of prototyping, but less confident about product judgment and narrowing choices.
- Assumption: no live browser pass or student analytics were used for this critique, so the read is based on the lesson manifest, shell code, workbench behavior, and surrounding product docs.
- Confidence: high on the structural critique because the route, workbench, completion model, and lesson contract are clear in code; medium on emotional/friction severity because this pass did not include observed student sessions.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The most important decision is visually framed as a quick pre-step instead of the core act of the lesson.

User cannot feel the weight of choosing a user, causing shallow commitment, because the handoff card is presented like a setup panel above the "real" sections instead of as the lesson's primary decision surface.

Impact:

- Students can click one of the three cards quickly without pressure-testing why that user is the right one.
- The lesson behaves like a form unlock mechanic instead of a commitment point.
- Weak user choices propagate directly into the outcome, research, and scope lessons.

### 2. The workbench says "the selected problem owner is your user," but it does not help the student validate that this person is actually reachable and worth serving.

User cannot confidently judge whether the chosen user is a good launch target, causing false certainty, because the current flow jumps straight from selection to three blank fields with no comparison or selection rubric.

Impact:

- "Reachability" becomes an afterthought instead of a go/no-go filter.
- Students may pick the most interesting problem, not the most testable user.
- The product loses one of its most commercially valuable constraints: proximity to real feedback.

### 3. The split-shell objective rail measures task completion, not decision quality.

User can see `0/4` or `4/4`, but not whether they have a tight user definition, causing checkbox behavior, because the left rail tracks fields and next unlocks rather than the quality bar for this lesson.

Impact:

- The page encourages "finish the form" rather than "choose the right user."
- Students may assume any filled text is good enough.
- Business-wise, the lesson does less to improve downstream artifact quality than it could.

### 4. The two section cards create a false sense of sequence clarity after the choice, but not before it.

User understands how to fill fields after selection, but not how to make the selection itself, causing early hesitation, because the strongest structure in the page is applied only to the follow-up sections and not to the choice that unlocks them.

Impact:

- The learner gets process help only after the highest-risk decision is already made.
- The "locked" treatment makes the form logic clear, but not the thinking logic.
- The best coaching moment in the lesson is under-instrumented.

### 5. Validation is inconsistent: `1.1` has lesson-specific completion coaching, but `1.2` falls back to generic failure language.

User cannot tell what kind of specificity is missing, causing avoidable correction loops, because `selected_problem_index`, `pain_moment`, `current_solution`, and `reachability` all inherit generic completion feedback.

Impact:

- Failed completion attempts feel bureaucratic.
- Students do not learn the standard for a strong user statement from the interface itself.
- The lesson underperforms as product coaching even when the data model is correct.

### 6. The lesson does not clearly show the artifact it is producing as a durable handoff into `1.3`.

User cannot easily understand what is being locked in, causing weaker trust in progress, because the screen talks about "User Statement" in shell chrome while the main workbench language is about a selected problem plus three support fields.

Impact:

- The artifact can feel abstract or invisible.
- The student may not realize that this exact framing will feed the next lessons and prompt builders.
- The product misses a chance to reinforce compounding value from each saved artifact.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Promote the user-selection handoff into a scored decision block.

What to change:

- Turn the current handoff card into a dedicated "Choose the user to build for" block with a short rubric under each candidate: pain is recurring, user is specific, I can reach them soon.
- Add one sentence under the block title: "This is the commitment that shapes the rest of the CEO block."
- After a user is selected, keep the other two candidates visible in a collapsed "Not chosen" state so the decision remains legible.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the choice feel consequential instead of mechanical.
- It helps the student evaluate the options before locking one in.

How it supports business growth:

- Better `1.2` choices improve the quality of all downstream artifacts.
- A clearer commitment moment strengthens perceived teaching quality and reduces confusion-driven churn.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Slightly more UI density in the upper workbench.
- If overdone, the scoring could feel like homework instead of guidance.

How to validate:

- Compare `1.2` completion quality before/after by sampling saved `reachability` and `pain_moment` fields.
- In usability tests, ask users why they picked their chosen user; success is when they can answer with the rubric language, not just instinct.

### 2. Add a pre-selection reality-check panel before the locked sections.

What to change:

- Insert a compact panel between the candidate cards and the locked sections titled `Pressure-test the choice`.
- Include three prompts: "When does this pain hit hardest?", "How are they solving it now?", and "Can you get this in front of them this week?"
- If no problem is selected yet, show these prompts as the decision criteria; once selected, restate them as the reason the next sections exist.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It clarifies the thinking sequence before the form unlocks.
- It bridges the decision from abstract candidate to testable user.

How it supports business growth:

- Increases the odds that students choose reachable users and therefore ship something testable faster.
- Reduces later-stage flailing caused by building for a vague audience.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Needs concise copy; otherwise it becomes another reading wall.
- Some of this overlaps with existing section instructions, so duplication needs to be intentional and brief.

How to validate:

- Track whether average completion time rises materially. If it does not, but downstream artifact quality improves, the extra framing is justified.
- Review support transcripts for a drop in "I do not know who to pick" style questions.

### 3. Reframe the left objective rail around the commitment, not the form.

What to change:

- Change the helper copy from generic completion framing to something like: "Leave this lesson with one reachable user in one painful moment."
- Rename the handoff task label from `Pick problem to carry forward` to `Commit to the user behind the problem`.
- Change the next-unlock line to a narrative handoff such as `Next: define the outcome for this user`.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It ties the chrome to the actual decision standard.
- It reminds the student that the lesson is about clarity, not field count.

How it supports business growth:

- Better framing should improve continuation into `1.3`.
- Narrative continuity strengthens trust that the course is a coherent system instead of a stack of forms.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Mostly copy and hierarchy work; it does not fix validation by itself.
- If the phrasing gets too abstract, it could become less actionable.

How to validate:

- Ask first-time users to explain what this lesson is for after 10 seconds on the page.
- Success is when they describe a user-commitment decision, not "fill out some boxes."

### 4. Add lesson-specific diagnostic validation for the `1.2` fields.

What to change:

- Extend `getLessonSpecificCompletionMessage` for `1.2`.
- Use targeted messages such as:
  - `selected_problem_index`: "Choose the one user-problem pair you can test fastest."
  - `pain_moment`: "Name the exact moment this becomes painful for them."
  - `current_solution`: "Describe the workaround they already use today."
  - `reachability`: "State how you can get this in front of them this week."
- Consider lightweight non-blocking inline hints once a field is touched but still empty or vague.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It turns completion errors into coaching.
- It teaches the quality bar inside the lesson instead of after failure.

How it supports business growth:

- Fewer failed completion attempts should improve momentum and perceived polish.
- Better field quality improves downstream prompt generation and artifact trust.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Any heuristic "vagueness" logic should stay advisory to avoid false negatives.
- Too much inline policing could make the lesson feel overbearing.

How to validate:

- Track failed completion attempts and post-error correction rates.
- Review whether saved artifacts become more concrete after the change.

### 5. Make the artifact visible as a composed user statement preview before completion.

What to change:

- Add a compact live preview card near the footer or below the sections that assembles the selected user, pain moment, current workaround, and reachability into one readable summary.
- Label it as the artifact being saved: `User Statement`.
- After completion, keep that preview visible in the celebration state with an explicit `Continue to 1.3` action.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It helps the student see what they have actually produced.
- It reinforces that the lesson creates a durable asset, not just internal form state.

How it supports business growth:

- Clearer progress and artifact ownership should improve continuation and perceived product value.
- It reduces support risk from students who do not understand what got saved.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Requires care to avoid duplicating too much of the pinned artifact system.
- The preview should be synthesis, not another large card wall.

How to validate:

- Test whether users can explain what got saved and what `1.3` will use next.
- Compare `1.2` to `1.3` clickthrough and revisit behavior after the preview is added.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

- Favor making the selection moment heavier, even if it adds a little more copy. This lesson is a decision gate, so underselling the choice is riskier than adding one concise rubric.
- Keep the split-shell architecture for now. The main issue is not layout mode itself; it is that the most important coaching is not attached tightly enough to the selection action.
- Prioritize diagnostic feedback and artifact clarity over visual polish. Those changes directly affect activation, continuity, and downstream artifact quality.
- Do not add complex scoring or branching logic yet. A lightweight rubric and better framing should be tried before any richer decision engine.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Reframe the handoff card as the main decision surface with a concise selection rubric.
- Add `1.2`-specific completion messages for the selected problem and each required field.
- Rewrite the objective rail helper/task/next-unlock copy around user commitment and downstream handoff.

### Should

- Add a compact pre-selection reality-check panel that explains how to choose among the three candidates.
- Add a live composed `User Statement` preview before completion and preserve it in the celebration state.

### Could

- Add light comparative indicators on all three candidate cards after selection so users can still see what they did not choose.
- Add advisory heuristics for overly broad reachability language once real student inputs are available to tune against.

## Validation Plan

- Run browser QA on the full `1.1 -> 1.2 -> 1.3` flow after the UX changes land, using a real saved `candidate_problems` artifact.
- Instrument `1.2` draft saves, completion attempts, completion failures, and clickthrough into `1.3`.
- Sample saved `1.2` artifacts weekly and grade them for specificity: named user, exact pain moment, real workaround, credible reachability path.
- In 5-user task testing, ask learners to pick one candidate and explain why. Success is when they use concrete user-selection reasoning rather than vague preference.
- Review whether `1.3` outcome statements become more concrete after `1.2` improves; that is the downstream proof that this lesson is doing its job.

## Narrative Summary

`1.2` is supposed to be the moment where the course stops being a pile of possible directions and becomes one chosen lane. The current implementation has good structure once a selection is made, but it under-frames the choice itself. The page is strongest at helping the student fill fields after they have already committed, and weaker at helping them make the commitment well.

The right move is not a visual redesign. It is to promote the selection into the main act of the lesson, define the quality bar in-product, and make the saved artifact visible enough that the student feels the compounding logic of the course. If that happens, `1.2` becomes a real narrowing step that improves everything after it instead of a brief gating form between `1.1` and `1.3`.
