# Lesson 1.6 UX Critique: Kill Scope

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to turn a paying student's messy idea into a shipped v1 over one guided weekend. The product succeeds when each lesson forces a clearer decision, creates a usable artifact, and protects momentum instead of letting students drift back into planning theater.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.6` needs to help the student reduce a mapped core flow into the smallest believable v1. This surface should make subtraction feel concrete, defensible, and fast enough that the student leaves with a sharper build boundary instead of another brainstorm.

### Business model

This is a paid digital product sold through Stripe and delivered through Clerk and Convex. Revenue quality depends on activation, lesson progression, trust in the guidance, and whether students feel the course is helping them ship something real instead of expanding the work.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.5` to `1.6` continuation rate
- `1.6` completion rate
- `1.6` to `1.7` continuation rate
- time from page load to first saved scope edit
- use rate of the optional AI scope import
- failed completion attempts on `1.6`
- support or refund signals around "what should I cut?" or "is this small enough?"
- downstream scope quality in `1.7` brief assembly and `3.1` build kickoff

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive here with a saved user statement, outcome statement, research notes, and core-flow image, but many still emotionally want to keep too much.
- Assumption: this lesson is strategically important because it determines whether the weekend sprint stays believable.
- Assumption: the critique is grounded in the coded lesson contract, workbench mode, prompt-import flow, and lesson shell behavior, not a live recording or analytics pull.
- Confidence: high on structural issues because the lesson contract and UI logic are explicit in code; medium on severity ranking because this pass did not include a live browser session.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson’s title and primary labels do not consistently express the student job.

User cannot instantly tell whether this lesson is about defining scope, cutting scope, or writing an MVP brief, causing comprehension drag, because the tracker and course ops call it `Kill Scope`, the lesson title is `Scope Hammer`, the objective title is `CEO 1.6 — Scope Hammer`, and the artifact is labeled `Scope Boundary`.

Impact:

- The page spends student attention on translation instead of action.
- The emotional energy of the lesson gets diluted at the moment the product should be most decisive.
- The inconsistency makes the flow feel less authored and lowers perceived sharpness.

### 2. The main workbench starts with an optional AI assist instead of the core judgment call.

User is nudged toward outsourcing the hardest decision, causing weaker ownership and lower-confidence scope choices, because `PromptImportCard` appears above the keep/cut boards and offers a generated first pass before the student has tightened the boundary themselves.

Impact:

- Students may paste a plausible answer without internalizing why those items stay or go.
- The course risks training dependence on generated framing when the product promise is stronger product judgment.
- Completion can become "import and lightly accept" rather than "decide and defend."

### 3. The lesson does not actively use the `1.5` core-flow artifact to drive cuts.

User cannot clearly see what they are cutting from, causing abstract or arbitrary scope decisions, because the prior core flow is only available as a pinned artifact in side context rather than being embedded into the active scope-cut workflow.

Impact:

- The keep/cut lists can drift into generic product planning.
- Students may keep platform features instead of steps required by the core flow.
- The causal bridge from `1.5` to `1.6` is weaker than it should be.

### 4. The system checks for filled fields, not for a credibly small v1.

User can complete the lesson without proving the scope is actually tight, causing false progress, because completion is satisfied when the keep list, cut list, and rationale are populated, not when the keep list is constrained or the rationale demonstrates a believable Sunday test.

Impact:

- Students can save six or ten "keep" items and still pass.
- The most important lesson in the CEO block becomes a form-completion step instead of a strategic filter.
- Weak scope boundaries will leak into later build lessons and inflate perceived course difficulty.

### 5. The UI lacks a strong in-product forcing function for subtraction.

User knows they should cut, but not how aggressively, causing over-retention of nice-to-have features, because the workbench offers placeholders and teaching points but no explicit counterweight like a keep-count cap, Sunday test prompt, or "delete one more thing" checkpoint in the active field flow.

Impact:

- The lesson teaches subtraction conceptually but does not operationalize it strongly enough in the interface.
- Students who are attached to their feature list can rationalize keeping too much.
- The business promise of a weekend v1 becomes easier to miss.

### 6. The rationale field comes after the lists, but the screen does not make the causal logic obvious.

User may treat `Why this version is enough` as generic reassurance instead of proof, causing weaker decision quality, because the page does not clearly frame the rationale as the defense for why this smaller version still delivers the promised outcome.

Impact:

- Rationales can become vague optimism instead of a compact argument.
- The student misses the chance to align user outcome, core flow, and scope.
- The later brief assembly gets a weaker scope narrative.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Standardize the lesson around one job-oriented label.

What to change:

- Pick one canonical student-facing name and use it everywhere in the live product. My recommendation is `Kill Scope`.
- Keep `Scope Boundary` as the artifact label if needed, but the page headline, objective title, tracker, and next-step references should all use the same lesson name.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It removes translation friction and makes the job obvious immediately.
- It gives the lesson a sharper behavioral command, which matters for this specific decision point.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger perceived polish improves trust in the product.
- Clearer lesson intent should support progression and reduce hesitation at the start of the task.

Trade-offs or risks:

- `Scope Hammer` has more brand flavor, so standardizing may reduce stylistic distinctiveness.
- If you keep the stronger metaphor internally, avoid leaking both names into the student surface.

How to validate:

- Ask QA users to describe the lesson goal after a three-second scan.
- Compare first-action time and hesitation notes before and after naming cleanup.

### 2. Move the AI assist below the manual scope decision path or collapse it harder.

What to change:

- Start the workbench with the keep/cut decision area and a tight "scope rules" strip.
- Move the optional AI starter below the manual fields, or keep it collapsed with lower visual weight and copy that says `Use only if you are stuck after a first pass`.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It keeps the student focused on judgment first.
- It preserves the AI helper as fallback instead of default behavior.

How it supports business growth:

- Better student ownership increases perceived course value.
- Stronger internalization should improve downstream lesson quality and reduce "the AI told me this" dependency.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Some students will move slower on the first attempt.
- A few weaker students may benefit from the current quick-start flow, so the assist should remain available, just deprioritized.

How to validate:

- Track AI import usage rate and compare completion quality of imported vs non-imported scopes.
- In QA, ask students whether they felt they made a scope decision or accepted one.

### 3. Make the prior core flow the active source material for this lesson.

What to change:

- Add a compact, always-visible context module above the fields showing:
  - `User`
  - `Promise`
  - `Core flow`
- For the core flow, surface the uploaded `1.5` artifact preview with one explicit line: `Keep only what this flow needs to reach the win.`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It anchors subtraction to the already-defined journey rather than abstract ambition.
- It turns `1.6` into a continuation of `1.5` instead of a fresh planning screen.

How it supports business growth:

- Better continuity makes the course feel more cumulative and intentional.
- Tighter scope boundaries improve the odds that students actually ship and attribute that result to the product.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Extra context can clutter the workbench if the preview is too large.
- The module needs to stay compact and decisional, not become another side panel.

How to validate:

- Review whether keep lists map more directly to saved core-flow steps after the change.
- Check whether `1.7` briefs show less generic feature sprawl.

### 4. Add a lightweight "brutally small" quality gate.

What to change:

- Add one explicit rule in the active UI: `Keep in v1: 3-5 items max`.
- Add one micro-check before completion, such as:
  - `Every keep item is required for the user to get the promised outcome`
  - `I cut at least three tempting extras`
  - `This can be built in a weekend without side systems`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It turns the lesson from freeform listing into an enforced scope decision.
- It provides a clearer definition of done without heavy bureaucracy.

How it supports business growth:

- Better scope discipline increases the odds of first-student success.
- It protects the core product promise of going from idea to v1 this weekend.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Any extra gate adds friction.
- Hard caps can feel arbitrary for edge cases, so frame them as launch rules rather than permanent truth.

How to validate:

- Track average keep-item count before and after the change.
- Watch whether later build lessons show fewer oversized plans and stalled starts.

### 5. Turn the rationale prompt into a proof prompt.

What to change:

- Relabel or support the rationale field with a stronger framing line:
  - `Why this smaller version still delivers the promise`
  - `What has to work by Sunday for the promise to still be true?`
- Add one hint that the rationale should reference the user, the outcome, and the shortest usable flow.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reframes the field as a decision defense, not filler text.
- It helps the student articulate why cutting is responsible, not lazy.

How it supports business growth:

- Better rationales improve later handoff quality in the brief and build stages.
- It reinforces the product’s core mechanism: product strategy, design, and build aligned around one outcome.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Slightly more directive copy can feel repetitive if overdone.
- Keep the hint compact so it clarifies without becoming another lecture.

How to validate:

- Review saved rationale quality for specificity and reference to promised outcome.
- In QA, ask whether students can explain why their v1 is enough in one sentence.

### 6. Add a post-completion or pre-next-step handoff that frames `1.7`.

What to change:

- In the completion footer or celebration state, add one line like:
  - `Next: turn this scope boundary into your CEO brief.`
- Make the locked artifact message speak to what was gained, not just what was saved.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the scope cut feel like a meaningful decision that will be reused immediately.
- It preserves momentum into the wrap-up lesson instead of making completion feel administrative.

How it supports business growth:

- Better lesson-to-lesson continuity supports activation and progression.
- It increases the sense that the course is compounding effort rather than asking for disconnected tasks.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too much footer copy can dilute the core completion state.
- Keep it to one sentence with explicit causal language.

How to validate:

- Measure `1.6` to `1.7` clickthrough and compare against earlier lessons.
- During QA, ask what the next lesson will do with the saved scope boundary.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

- The biggest product decision here is whether `1.6` should optimize for speed or for judgment quality. My recommendation is to bias toward judgment quality with only light added friction, because this lesson has outsized downstream impact.
- The optional AI assist is useful, but the current hierarchy over-privileges it. Keep it as a rescue tool, not the headline path.
- A hard quality gate should stay lightweight. Do not turn this lesson into a rubric. A clear keep-count ceiling plus one or two strategic confirmations is enough.
- The strongest leverage is not visual polish. It is making the screen operationalize subtraction using the prior core flow and promised outcome.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Standardize the lesson naming across the student surface.
- Move or de-emphasize the AI assist so manual scoping happens first.
- Surface the `1.5` core flow, chosen user, and promised outcome directly in the active workbench.
- Add one explicit "small enough for Sunday" quality rule before completion.

### Should

- Reframe the rationale field as proof that the smaller version still delivers the promise.
- Improve the completion state so it clearly hands off into `1.7`.

### Could

- Add a tiny inline counter or visual warning when the keep list exceeds the recommended maximum.
- Add a lesson-specific example card showing oversized scope vs believable Sunday scope inside the workbench.

## Validation Plan

- Run browser QA on `1.5 -> 1.6 -> 1.7` and watch whether the student can explain what they are cutting from and why.
- Measure median keep-item count and the share of lessons completed with more than five keep items.
- Compare `1.6` completion quality between students who use the AI import and those who do not.
- Review a sample of saved rationales for whether they reference the user, outcome, and Sunday-sized scope.
- Track failed completion attempts and support notes tied to "is this small enough?" confusion.
- In a live QA session, ask the student two questions before they continue:
  - `What did you cut and why?`
  - `Why is this still enough for the user to get the win?`

## Narrative Summary

`1.6` is one of the most important lessons in the whole product because it determines whether the student is actually preparing to ship or quietly rebuilding the fantasy app with better wording. The current screen has the right raw pieces: keep list, cut list, rationale, prior artifacts, and an optional AI starter. But it is still too easy to complete the lesson without making the real decision. The interface does not force the student to scope against the saved core flow strongly enough, and it lets AI assistance take visual priority over personal judgment.

The recommended direction is to make the lesson harsher in the right way, not heavier. Put the manual scope boundary first. Bring the chosen user, promised outcome, and core flow into the active workbench. Add one explicit "this must fit in a weekend" rule. Reframe the rationale as proof. That would make the lesson feel more like a decisive product-management move and less like another editable form. If this lesson gets sharper, the rest of the course gets easier to trust because the student is now carrying a buildable v1 instead of an attractive overreach.
