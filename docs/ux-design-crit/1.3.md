# Lesson 1.3 UX Critique: Define the Outcome

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to move a paying student from a vague product instinct to a shipped v1 over a guided weekend sprint. The product works when each lesson reduces ambiguity, produces a durable artifact, and increases confidence that the next step is the right one.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.3` needs to turn the chosen user from `1.2` into one clear promised outcome the student can build and test. This page should help the student define a result, not a feature list, and set up the evidence they will look for in `1.4`.

### Business model

This is a paid digital course sold through Stripe with access handled through Clerk and Convex. Revenue quality depends on activation after purchase, continued lesson progression, perceived instructional quality, and low refund/support risk from students who feel the sprint is coherent and actionable.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.2` to `1.3` continuation rate
- `1.3` completion rate
- `1.3` to `1.4` progression rate
- Failed completion attempts on `1.3`
- Time to first saved draft in `1.3`
- Downstream quality of `1.4` research inputs and later demo scope
- Support signals around "what counts as an outcome?" or "is this too feature-heavy?"

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive with a saved `user_statement`, but many still think in terms of app features rather than user-visible change.
- Assumption: this lesson is strategically important because it sets the promise that later research, scope, and build decisions are supposed to support.
- Assumption: this critique is based on repo code, lesson config, and product docs rather than live student session recordings or analytics.
- Confidence: high on structural findings because the lesson manifest, completion logic, and shared lesson shell are clear in code; medium on severity ranking because this pass did not include a live browser walkthrough.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson asks for one promised outcome, but the three fields blur into overlapping writing prompts.

User cannot tell the difference between the main outcome, what success looks like, and what will be different for the user, causing repetitive answers and weak judgment, because the page presents three adjacent text areas without clearly defining the distinct role of each one.

Impact:

- Students may write the same sentence three times with minor wording changes.
- The artifact becomes verbose without becoming clearer.
- Downstream lessons inherit a mushy promise instead of a crisp success condition.

### 2. The page warns against feature language, but it does not teach the rewrite in the moment of action.

User cannot confidently convert a feature idea into an outcome statement, causing hesitation or shallow feature-led answers, because the strongest instructional line is in the section copy while examples and teaching points remain outside the input moment.

Impact:

- Students can understand the slogan "People buy outcomes, not tools" without knowing how to apply it.
- The lesson risks producing outputs like "an app that summarizes meetings faster" instead of a user-visible result.
- Perceived teaching quality drops if the course sounds clear but does not help the student execute.

### 3. The prior user context is visible, but the page never synthesizes the promise into a single testable statement.

User cannot easily see the artifact they are actually creating, causing weaker trust in progress, because the pinned `user_statement` context sits beside three raw fields and there is no live assembled preview of the promised outcome.

Impact:

- Students may not know which sentence should guide later research and demo choices.
- The lesson feels like worksheet fill-in rather than strategic positioning.
- The product misses a chance to reinforce artifact compounding across lessons.

### 4. Validation is generic where this lesson needs judgment coaching.

User cannot tell whether their problem is emptiness, vagueness, or feature framing, causing avoidable retry loops, because `1.3` falls back to generic required-field feedback instead of lesson-specific messages while `1.2` already has targeted completion coaching in `registry.ts`.

Impact:

- Failed completion attempts feel administrative.
- The interface teaches less than it could at the exact point where judgment is still forming.
- The lesson underperforms as a product strategy coach even when the gating logic works.

### 5. The workbench frames progress as field completion, not promise clarity.

User can see progress counts, but not whether the outcome is observable and strong enough to carry forward, because the status rail and objective panel emphasize `3/3` completion and artifact labels rather than the standard for a good promise.

Impact:

- Students can optimize for filling boxes instead of sharpening the product promise.
- The lesson’s strategic value gets flattened into mechanical completion.
- Business-wise, weaker outcome definition can degrade later research quality and demo coherence.

### 6. The handoff to `1.4` is implied in copy but not made operational.

User cannot see how this outcome will be pressure-tested next, causing a weaker sense of narrative continuity, because the teaching points mention tying the outcome to a later demo, but the page does not show what `1.4` will validate or how this artifact will be used.

Impact:

- The student may treat the outcome as a writing exercise instead of a hypothesis.
- Continuation into research is less motivated than it could be.
- The course loses some compounding momentum between CEO lessons.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Reframe the three fields as a clear promise ladder.

What to change:

- Rename the fields and section copy so each one has a distinct job:
  - `Outcome statement`: the single promise
  - `Observable proof`: what the user can now do
  - `Pain removed`: what no longer hurts
- Add one short line above the evidence section explaining that the second and third fields exist to pressure-test the first field, not repeat it.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces semantic overlap and gives the student a clearer writing sequence.
- It helps the student understand what belongs in each box.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger outcome artifacts improve the quality of later research, scope, and demo decisions.
- Clearer structure reduces confusion-driven friction in a paid product.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Mostly copy and information-architecture work, so it will not fix weak examples by itself.
- The labels need to stay plain enough to avoid sounding academic.

How to validate:

- Review saved `1.3` artifacts and check whether the three fields become less repetitive.
- In usability testing, ask students to explain the difference between the three prompts before they start writing.

### 2. Add inline weak-to-strong outcome examples directly above the first field.

What to change:

- Put one weak example and one rewritten strong example inside the `Write the promised outcome` section.
- Use the actual lesson rule: result for the user, not feature inventory.
- Keep the coach rail examples, but make sure the essential rewrite pattern is visible without opening another panel.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It teaches the transformation from feature idea to user outcome at the exact moment the student needs it.
- It reduces the cognitive gap between principle and execution.

How it supports business growth:

- Better first-pass responses should improve completion and continuation.
- Stronger instructional clarity supports perceived product quality and lowers support burden.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too many inline examples could narrow students into mimicry.
- The examples should demonstrate the pattern, not prescribe the answer.

How to validate:

- Compare first-draft quality before and after the inline examples ship.
- Watch whether students rely less on trial-and-error phrasing to get started.

### 3. Add a live "promised outcome" preview that synthesizes the prior user statement and current draft.

What to change:

- Show a compact preview card below the first section or near the footer:
  - `For <chosen user>, after using this, they can <outcome>.`
  - Then show the evidence lines beneath it.
- Label it clearly as the artifact being saved: `Outcome Statement`.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the artifact legible and durable.
- It helps the student judge whether the promise sounds concrete enough to research and build.

How it supports business growth:

- Better artifact visibility strengthens momentum and trust in the lesson system.
- Students are more likely to understand that each lesson compounds into the next, which supports retention through the sprint.

Trade-offs or risks:

- It may duplicate part of the existing artifact-preview pattern if implemented carelessly.
- The preview should synthesize, not create another heavy card.

How to validate:

- Ask users what exactly got saved at the end of `1.3`.
- Success is when they can restate the promise in one sentence without rereading the whole page.

### 4. Add lesson-specific diagnostic validation for `1.3`.

What to change:

- Extend `getLessonSpecificCompletionMessage` in `lib/course/registry.ts` for:
  - `outcome`: "Rewrite this as what changes for the user, not what the product contains."
  - `success_looks_like`: "Show what the user can now do or finish when this works."
  - `difference_for_user`: "Describe what pain disappears or gets easier for them."
- Consider lightweight non-blocking hints if the outcome field contains obvious feature language like "dashboard", "platform", or "tool".

Why it solves the user problem:

- It turns the completion gate into coaching.
- It teaches the lesson standard inside the product instead of after a failed guess.

How it supports business growth:

- Fewer failed attempts and better artifact quality should improve progression into `1.4`.
- Better guidance reduces support questions around basic product-thinking concepts.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Feature-language heuristics can overfire, so they should be advisory first.
- Too much inline correction could feel nagging if it interrupts flow.

How to validate:

- Track failed completion attempts on `1.3` and measure whether they drop.
- Sample saved artifacts to see whether outcome statements become less feature-led.

### 5. Make the `1.4` handoff explicit: this promise will now be pressure-tested.

What to change:

- Update the objective/helper copy and footer celebration copy to say that `1.4` is where the student will find evidence for or against this promised outcome.
- Replace generic `Next unlock` framing with a narrative handoff such as `Next: find evidence that this outcome matters in the real world.`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reframes the lesson output as a hypothesis that will be checked next, not just a sentence to save.
- It increases continuity across the CEO block.

How it supports business growth:

- Clearer narrative sequencing should improve continuation and reduce the feeling of worksheet churn.
- The course feels more like a guided product process, which supports retention and referrals.

Trade-offs or risks:

- This is mostly copy and framing work; it should not be mistaken for deeper validation support.
- The handoff language needs to stay concrete or it will feel like abstract motivation copy.

How to validate:

- Ask users what `1.4` is for immediately after finishing `1.3`.
- Success is when they describe evidence-gathering for the promised outcome, not generic "research."

## Trade-offs and Decisions

- Favor clarity over adding more fields. The core issue is not lack of data capture; it is that the current prompts do not create a crisp mental model for the artifact.
- Keep the shared `guided_form` architecture for now. The immediate leverage is in lesson-specific framing, preview, and validation, not a new workbench mode.
- Prioritize inline teaching and diagnostic feedback before visual polish. This lesson is a judgment lesson, so instruction quality matters more than cosmetic refinement.
- Treat the `1.4` handoff as part of the UX fix. A stronger narrative chain makes this lesson feel consequential without making it more complex.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Reframe the three prompts so they have clearly different jobs.
- Add lesson-specific validation messages for `1.3`.
- Add one inline weak-to-strong outcome example in the first section.

### Should

- Add a live synthesized `Outcome Statement` preview.
- Rewrite objective rail and completion copy around the promise being tested next in `1.4`.

### Could

- Add advisory feature-language heuristics to flag obviously weak outcome drafts.
- Add a compact "what a good outcome includes" rubric near the first field if the inline examples alone are not enough.

## Validation Plan

- Run a browser QA pass on `1.3` and verify that a first-time student can explain each field’s role before writing.
- Compare failed completion attempts and time-to-complete before and after the validation/copy changes.
- Sample saved outcome artifacts for repetition rate and feature-language frequency.
- In 5-user task testing, ask:
  - "What is the one promise you are making?"
  - "How is `success looks like` different from `difference for the user`?"
  - "What will the next lesson do with this?"
- Watch downstream `1.3` to `1.4` continuation and whether `1.4` research entries reference the promised outcome more directly.

## Narrative Summary

Lesson `1.3` is strategically important because it sets the promise the rest of the product work is supposed to support. The current implementation has the right intent, but it behaves too much like three required writing boxes and not enough like a coached promise-definition step. The biggest gap is not missing UI chrome; it is missing precision. Students need a clearer distinction between the main outcome, the proof of success, and the pain removed, plus in-the-moment help converting feature thoughts into user-visible change.

The recommended path is to keep the existing `guided_form` structure and strengthen the lesson-specific coaching around it. Clarify the field roles, add inline weak-to-strong examples, synthesize the artifact into a visible preview, and make validation messages teach the outcome standard instead of just enforcing required fields. Then tie the finish state directly into `1.4` as an evidence-gathering step. That combination should make the lesson feel more like product coaching and less like worksheet completion, which is the right trade for both student momentum and business retention.
