# Lesson 1.4 UX Critique: Light Research, Not a Thesis

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` needs to get a paying student from an initial product instinct to a shipped v1 over a guided weekend sprint. The course works when each lesson produces a usable artifact, sharpens the next decision, and keeps the student moving without falling into side quests.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.4` needs to help the student collect just enough outside signal to pressure-test the user and outcome chosen in `1.2` and `1.3`. This page should produce decision-useful research notes that make the next scope and flow decisions better, not create a faux research project.

### Business model

This is a paid digital course sold through Stripe with product access handled through Clerk and Convex. Revenue quality depends on post-purchase activation, lesson-to-lesson progression, perceived instructional sharpness, and low refund or support risk from students who feel the sprint is concrete and trustworthy.

### Business metrics that matter here

- `1.3` to `1.4` continuation rate
- `1.4` completion rate
- `1.4` to `1.5` progression rate
- Time to first saved research row
- Failed completion attempts on `1.4`
- Share of students who use AI assist vs manual entry
- Downstream quality of `1.5` core-flow decisions and `1.6` scope cuts
- Support signals around "what counts as good research?" or "am I supposed to trust the AI output?"

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: students arrive with a chosen user and a promised outcome, but many are still prone to over-research or to outsource judgment to AI too early.
- Assumption: this lesson is strategically important because it sets the evidence base for the rest of the CEO block, especially flow mapping and scope cutting.
- Assumption: this critique is based on repo code, lesson contracts, and shared workbench behavior rather than live analytics or observed session recordings.
- Confidence: high on the structural critique because the lesson manifest, split-shell behavior, prompt-import loop, and completion model are explicit in code; medium on friction severity because this pass did not include a live browser run.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson says "research lightly," but the page does not define what makes a research note trustworthy.

User cannot tell the difference between observed evidence, inference, and guesswork, causing low-trust or low-quality notes, because the table only asks for solution judgments while the UI never asks what source was reviewed or how confident the student should be in each row.

Impact:

- Students can fill the table with plausible-sounding claims that are hard to verify later.
- The product teaches opinion capture more than evidence gathering.
- Downstream scope decisions can feel arbitrary, which increases support and refund risk.

### 2. The optional AI assist is framed as optional in copy, but it is positioned like a co-primary workflow.

User cannot immediately tell whether they should do quick manual scanning first or jump to generation, causing mode confusion, because the workbench puts four prompt actions in a dense control cluster above the table and gives AI generation equal or greater operational salience than the manual path.

Impact:

- Students may outsource the core thinking step before they have looked at the market themselves.
- The lesson risks becoming "paste in AI output and tidy it" instead of "notice patterns and make decisions."
- Trust drops if the student is unsure whether the course wants their judgment or the model's shortcut.

### 3. The table captures row-by-row notes, but it does not help the student compare across rows in service of the chosen outcome.

User cannot easily answer "what does this mean for my product?" causing weak synthesis, because the table is structured around isolated entries and the only synthesis field is a single generic summary box at the end.

Impact:

- Students can finish with three rows and still not know what pattern to act on.
- The lesson produces notes, but not necessarily a clearer product decision.
- The next lessons inherit raw observations rather than a sharper point of view.

### 4. The lesson’s artifact is not visibly tied back to the user and outcome it is supposed to pressure-test.

User cannot keep the research anchored to the selected user and promise, causing drift into generic competitor notes, because the pinned artifacts live in adjacent shell chrome and the main workbench does not actively restate the target user/outcome inside the research action itself.

Impact:

- Rows can become broad category notes instead of evidence for this specific product bet.
- The student has to remember the framing instead of being coached by it.
- Business-wise, weaker anchoring lowers the quality of later flow and scope outputs.

### 5. Completion logic rewards "2-3 rows plus one summary" even when the rows are shallow or redundant.

User can complete the lesson without proving decision-useful insight, causing checkbox behavior, because the win condition and objective rail emphasize count-based completion rather than whether the research changed or clarified the product direction.

Impact:

- Students can optimize for finishing the page instead of learning from it.
- The lesson may feel administratively complete but strategically thin.
- The course loses some of its value as a product judgment system.

### 6. The handoff into `1.5` is too generic for a lesson that is supposed to change the next decision.

User cannot clearly see how research becomes a core flow, causing a weak sense of narrative payoff, because the current shell framing shows the next unlock but does not explain what part of the research should shape the next lesson.

Impact:

- The student may treat research as a side requirement instead of an input to design.
- Progression into `1.5` is less motivated than it could be.
- The CEO block feels more like adjacent worksheets than one compounding process.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Add an evidence standard directly into the table workflow.

What to change:

- Add a lightweight evidence signal to each row, either as a new compact column like `Evidence seen` or as helper text that forces one concrete reference type per entry such as product page, review thread, onboarding flow, or pricing page.
- Pair it with a short rule above the table: "Write what you can actually observe. If you are inferring, say it briefly."

Why it solves the user problem:

- It teaches students how to gather usable signal instead of writing market opinions.
- It makes each row easier to trust and easier to use later.

How it supports business growth:

- Better evidence quality improves downstream lesson quality and reduces trust-breaking ambiguity.
- It strengthens the course's credibility as a practical product process rather than generic startup advice.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Adding another field can increase perceived effort if the copy is too heavy.
- The evidence cue should stay lightweight so it does not turn into full citation homework.

How to validate:

- Sample saved `1.4` artifacts and measure whether rows reference observable sources more often.
- Ask users to explain why they believe one row after finishing the lesson; success is when they can point to what they actually checked.

### 2. Demote AI assist from "co-primary path" to "fallback accelerator."

What to change:

- Keep the AI import feature, but visually subordinate it under a clearer manual-first headline such as `Need a fast first pass?`
- Reorder the card so the default guidance is:
  1. scan 2-3 real solutions
  2. jot rows manually
  3. use AI only if you are stuck or want a starter draft
- Reduce the initial button clutter by hiding prompt and paste actions behind one `Use AI starter` control.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the intended behavior obvious: judgment first, acceleration second.
- It reduces the chance that students skip the actual learning move.

How it supports business growth:

- Preserves the helpfulness of AI without letting it hollow out the course's core value.
- Better student ownership of the artifact should improve trust and continuation.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Power users may want the fast path immediately.
- The fallback path still needs to stay discoverable and fast once invoked.

How to validate:

- Track manual-entry-first behavior vs immediate generation behavior.
- Review whether AI-generated rows require less cleanup once the prompt is used later in the flow rather than first.

### 3. Turn the summary field into a decision synthesis, not a generic pattern note.

What to change:

- Rename `Key pattern I noticed` to something more operational, such as `What this means for my v1`.
- Add a structured prompt beneath it: `Across these options, what should I copy, avoid, or differentiate on for this user and outcome?`
- Consider splitting the synthesis into two short prompts if needed:
  - `Pattern across solutions`
  - `Decision for my v1`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It converts research notes into a product point of view.
- It makes the lesson output more useful for `1.5` and `1.6`.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger synthesis increases the practical value students feel from the lesson.
- Better handoff quality should improve downstream progression and reduce support confusion.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Adding too much structure could overcomplicate an intentionally light lesson.
- Keep the synthesis compact so it still feels like a fast pass.

How to validate:

- Review saved summaries and check whether they contain action-oriented design or scope implications.
- In usability testing, ask what they would do differently after this lesson; success is when they can answer without rereading the rows.

### 4. Re-anchor the workbench to the specific user and promised outcome inside the main action area.

What to change:

- Add a compact context strip above the table inside the workbench:
  - `Researching for: <user>`
  - `Promise to pressure-test: <outcome>`
- Add one sentence of instruction: `Look for evidence that this outcome matters and how current options handle it.`

Why it solves the user problem:

- It keeps the research narrow and relevant.
- It reduces drift into generic category exploration.

How it supports business growth:

- Better anchoring improves downstream artifact quality and reinforces the compounding lesson system.
- It makes the product feel more guided and intentional.

Trade-offs or risks:

- Some of this context already exists in pinned artifacts, so duplication should be concise.
- The strip should summarize, not add another heavy card.

How to validate:

- Compare saved rows before and after this change for relevance to the stated user and outcome.
- Ask users mid-lesson who the research is for; success is when they can answer instantly.

### 5. Make the success state about clarity gained, not just rows saved.

What to change:

- Update helper copy, win condition, and footer completion language to emphasize that the lesson is complete when the student can explain the market pattern that should influence the product.
- Add lesson-specific completion feedback in `lib/course/registry.ts` for:
  - `research_table`: "Add 2-3 real alternatives or workarounds and note what each reveals."
  - `research_summary`: "State the pattern that should change what you build, copy, or avoid."

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the completion gate teach the quality bar.
- It reduces the chance that students mistake volume for insight.

How it supports business growth:

- Better completion coaching should improve the quality of saved artifacts and the clarity of later lessons.
- The product feels more like expert guidance and less like a basic form app.

Trade-offs or risks:

- This adds instructional pressure, so it should stay lightweight and concrete.
- It will not solve evidence quality alone unless paired with the earlier recommendation.

How to validate:

- Track failed completion attempts and sample whether saved summaries become more decision-oriented.
- Watch `1.4` to `1.5` progression and ask whether students can name the design implication of the research.

### 6. Make the `1.5` handoff explicit: research now shapes the flow.

What to change:

- Replace generic next-step framing with narrative copy such as: `Next: map the core flow that keeps the best parts and avoids the common breakdowns you just found.`
- Reflect the same idea in the celebration or completion state.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It explains why the research mattered immediately.
- It gives the student a more compelling reason to continue.

How it supports business growth:

- Stronger narrative continuity should improve progression and perceived course coherence.
- The sprint feels like one guided system rather than disconnected exercises.

Trade-offs or risks:

- This is a framing fix, not a deeper behavior change by itself.
- The copy needs to stay specific or it will read like motivational filler.

How to validate:

- Ask users what `1.5` is for right after completing `1.4`.
- Success is when they say it is about shaping the flow based on what they learned from the market.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

- Favor better judgment scaffolding over more research depth. The problem is not missing analysis power; it is weak guidance around what "good enough" research means.
- Keep the `score_table` mode. The main leverage is in field framing, summary design, and AI-workflow hierarchy, not in inventing a new lesson architecture.
- Preserve AI assist, but position it as acceleration after grounding, not as the default move. The business win comes from helpfulness without reducing student ownership.
- Prioritize anchoring and synthesis before visual polish. This lesson is about turning signal into product decisions, so the UX should optimize for relevance and clarity first.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Add lesson-specific completion guidance for `research_table` and `research_summary`.
- Re-anchor the workbench to the chosen user and promised outcome inside the main content area.
- Reframe the summary field so it captures a product decision, not just a vague pattern.
- Rewrite the `1.5` handoff copy to explain how research informs the next lesson.

### Should

- Add a lightweight evidence cue per research row.
- Demote AI assist into a clearly secondary, fallback workflow.

### Could

- Add a more explicit comparison aid in the summary area, such as copy/avoid/differentiate prompts.
- Add analytics segmentation for manual-first vs AI-first completion paths.

- Compare saved `research_notes` artifacts before and after the updates for:
  - evidence specificity
  - relevance to the chosen user/outcome
  - actionability of the summary
- Track:

  - `1.4` to `1.5` progression

- In usability sessions, ask:
  - "What counts as good enough research here?"

  - "What are you doing in `1.5` because of this lesson?"

## Narrative Summary

Lesson `1.4` has the right strategic job: stop students from building in a vacuum without letting them disappear into research. The current implementation gets close structurally, but it still behaves more like a table plus an AI utility than a guided evidence-to-decision step. Students can complete it with plausible notes and a generic pattern statement without clearly proving what they learned or how it should affect the product.

The recommended path is to keep the existing split-shell and `score_table` architecture, then sharpen the lesson-specific coaching around it. Make the user and outcome explicit inside the workbench, teach a lightweight evidence standard, turn the summary into a product decision, and visually demote AI assist into a fallback accelerator. Then make the `1.5` handoff explicit: this research matters because it should change the flow they map next. That combination preserves speed while making the lesson feel more trustworthy, more decision-useful, and more aligned with the business value of a paid guided sprint.
