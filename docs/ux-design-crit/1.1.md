# Lesson 1.1 UX Critique: Start With the Problem

## Outcome Context

### Product goal

`Ship by Sunday` helps a paying student go from vague idea to shipped v1 in a single guided weekend sprint. The product only works if students get into motion quickly, trust the sequence, and keep producing artifacts that unlock the next step.

### Surface goal

Lesson `1.1` needs to get a newly onboarded student out of "AI app idea" mode and into three grounded candidate problems they can carry into the rest of the CEO block. The lesson should lower ambiguity, build confidence early, and create a clean handoff into `1.2`.

### Business model

This is a paid digital course product sold through Stripe, with access handled through Clerk and Convex. Revenue depends on conversion, activation after purchase, progress through the course, and reduced refund/support risk from confused students.

### Business metrics that matter here

- Activation rate from `Mission` into first CEO artifact completion
- Lesson `1.1` completion rate
- Progression rate from `1.1` to `1.2`
- Time to first saved artifact
- Support / confusion signals around "what should I write here?"
- Retention into the rest of the CEO block

### Assumptions and confidence

- Assumption: `1.1` is one of the first truly effortful student moments after the protected `Start Here` lessons, so it is carrying a disproportionate amount of activation weight.
- Assumption: most students are capable builders, but not always confident product thinkers.
- Assumption: this lesson currently runs without a real attached lesson video, based on the shared `LessonVideoSurface` placeholder behavior.
- Confidence: medium-high on structure and interaction details because the critique is grounded in repo code, lesson config, and product docs; medium on emotional response because this pass did not include live browser testing or student session data.

## Problems I Notice

### 1. The lesson asks for three candidate problems, but it does not help the student compare quality while writing them.

User cannot confidently tell whether each problem is "good enough," causing hesitation and shallow answers, because the workbench is structured as three repetitive write-only sections with examples and coaching pushed off to a separate rail.

Impact:

- Students can fill the form mechanically without improving judgment.
- Weak artifacts propagate into `1.2` and the rest of the CEO block.
- Early trust in the course mechanism drops if the first real output feels arbitrary.

### 2. The system hides the highest-value coaching away from the moment of action.

User cannot easily apply examples while filling the form, causing extra scanning and context switching, because the split shell puts action in the center while examples/resources/transcript live in a separate coach panel that can be closed or ignored.

Impact:

- First-time students may not notice the examples at all.
- The lesson creates unnecessary effort at the exact point where momentum matters most.
- The product underuses its instructional leverage in the activation step.

### 3. Feedback arrives too late and too generically.

User cannot correct mistakes early, causing frustration at completion time, because validation emphasis appears mainly when the learner tries to complete the lesson and the inline error language is generic: "Finish this before continuing."

Impact:

- The student does not know whether they are missing specificity, grounding, or just an empty field.
- The completion gate feels bureaucratic instead of instructional.
- The lesson may feel stricter than it feels helpful.

### 4. The page frames progress as form completion, not decision quality.

User can see that they have filled tasks, but not whether they are converging on a strong direction, because the objective rail focuses on counts, output labels, and next unlocks rather than the actual decision standard for this lesson.

Impact:

- Students may optimize for "get to 3/3" rather than "generate 3 real pains worth carrying forward."
- The product risks turning a strategy lesson into a paperwork step.
- Business-wise, this can reduce later lesson quality and completion.

### 5. Completion feedback resolves too fast to reinforce progress.

User barely has time to absorb what was locked in before being moved onward, causing weak accomplishment and orientation, because the page auto-redirects to the next lesson after a short celebration window.

Impact:

- Students lose a clear "I did the right thing" moment.
- The artifact does not get reinforced as a valuable asset.
- The transition into `1.2` feels system-driven instead of student-driven.

### 6. Missing lesson media weakens confidence in the guided format.

User sees a "video placeholder" state without a stronger alternative explanation, causing trust drag, because the interface reserves meaningful surface area for coaching even when the primary media is not attached.

Impact:

- The lesson can feel unfinished even if the workbook path is usable.
- Paid-course expectations and perceived product quality take a hit.
- This is especially risky in the first heavy-lift lesson after purchase.

## Solutions I Recommend

### 1. Turn the lesson into a guided comparison exercise, not three isolated text captures.

What to change:

- Add a compact "what makes a strong problem" rubric directly above the first section.
- After each candidate problem, show a short self-check with 3 criteria: real person, recurring pain, recognizable situation.
- Add a final summary strip after candidate 3 that visually compares all three entries side by side before completion.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It gives the student a quality bar while they write, not after they are done.
- It converts the lesson from blank-form anxiety into coached judgment-building.

Business impact:

- Better candidate problems should improve progression quality into `1.2`.
- Stronger early confidence should improve activation and reduce student confusion.

Likely metric movement:

- Higher `1.1` completion rate
- Better `1.1` -> `1.2` progression
- Lower support friction around what counts as a "good problem"

Trade-offs or risks:

- Adds some UI density.
- Needs restraint so the rubric does not become another heavy reading block.

How to validate:

- Compare completion rate and time-to-complete before/after.
- Run 5-user task testing and ask whether they can explain why one candidate is stronger than another.

### 2. Pull examples inline at the point of entry.

What to change:

- For `1.1`, surface one strong example and one weak-example contrast inside each section card or in a sticky inline helper block above the fields.
- Keep the right-side coach rail, but do not make it the primary place where essential examples live.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It reduces scanning distance between instruction and execution.
- It helps students translate the abstract lesson into a concrete answer format.

Business impact:

- Faster first artifact creation improves activation.
- Stronger perceived teaching quality supports retention and referral confidence.

Likely metric movement:

- Lower abandonment within `1.1`
- Faster time to first meaningful draft save

Trade-offs or risks:

- Too many examples inline could bias students toward copycat answers.
- The fix should demonstrate the pattern, not hand them the answer.

How to validate:

- Browser test with new users: watch whether they open coach panels less often while producing stronger outputs.
- Track whether drafts become longer and more specific, not just more numerous.

### 3. Replace generic completion errors with diagnostic coaching.

What to change:

- Add field-specific feedback language tied to the actual lesson standard.
- Example: instead of "Finish this before continuing," say "Name the person more specifically" or "Rewrite this as a repeated pain, not a product idea."
- Show lightweight incomplete-state hints before the user presses complete, especially when a field is non-empty but still weak by simple heuristics.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the system feel like a coach, not a gatekeeper.
- It helps the student improve the artifact instead of just satisfying required fields.

Business impact:

- Better UX in the first serious lesson lowers frustration and support burden.
- Better artifacts increase the odds that the rest of the course feels coherent.

Likely metric movement:

- Fewer failed completion attempts
- Higher satisfaction with the first block

Trade-offs or risks:

- Heuristic quality checks can be wrong if implemented too aggressively.
- Keep early checks advisory, not blocking, unless the case is obvious.

How to validate:

- Track completion-attempt failure rate.
- Review whether students revise weaker statements after seeing the new coaching.

### 4. Reframe the objective rail around the decision being made.

What to change:

- Rename the objective panel copy from a generic task list into a clearer decision frame such as "Leave this lesson with 3 real candidate pains."
- Add one line explaining how this artifact will be used in `1.2`.
- Change "Next unlock" from a mechanical system label into a handoff label such as "Next: choose the best user to carry forward."

Why it solves the user problem:

- It makes the page feel like a purposeful sequence, not a gated checklist.
- It clarifies why this work matters now.

Business impact:

- Better narrative continuity increases commitment to the next lesson.
- Students are more likely to trust the overall sprint structure.

Likely metric movement:

- Stronger continuation into `1.2`
- Better perceived clarity of the course journey

Trade-offs or risks:

- This is copy and information hierarchy work; it will not fix weak validation on its own.

How to validate:

- Ask test users what this lesson is for and what happens next.
- Success is when they can answer both without reading secondary docs.

### 5. Let the student own the transition after completion.

What to change:

- After successful completion, keep the celebration state visible and offer two actions: `Continue to 1.2` and `Review saved artifact`.
- Remove the forced auto-advance for guided-form lessons, or extend it only when the user is idle and the next step is obvious.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It gives the student a moment of certainty and control.
- It reinforces that they produced a durable artifact, not just unlocked the next screen.

Business impact:

- Better felt progress improves motivation to continue.
- Lower disorientation should reduce friction in early-course navigation.

Likely metric movement:

- Higher next-step clickthrough confidence
- Better self-reported progress clarity

Trade-offs or risks:

- Adds one more click for fast-moving users.
- Can be mitigated by making `Continue to 1.2` the clear primary action.

How to validate:

- Watch whether users pause to read the celebration and understand what they created.
- Compare drop-off immediately after completion.

### 6. Treat missing video as an intentional fallback state, not a placeholder.

What to change:

- Replace "video placeholder" language with a stronger fallback message: "No lesson video yet. You can complete this lesson fully using the guided examples and worksheet."
- Promote the worksheet and examples more aggressively when video is absent.

Why it solves the user problem:

- It preserves trust by framing the non-video path as valid and deliberate.
- It prevents the experience from feeling unfinished during launch.

Business impact:

- Protects perceived course quality before all media is attached.
- Reduces early refund-risk signals tied to incomplete-feeling delivery.

Likely metric movement:

- Better completion despite missing media
- Lower early trust erosion

Trade-offs or risks:

- This should not become an excuse to leave video missing long-term.

How to validate:

- Test with a fresh user and ask whether the lesson feels complete enough to proceed with confidence.

## Trade-offs and Decisions

### Best decision now

Prioritize learning-confidence fixes over visual polish. The biggest problem in `1.1` is not aesthetics; it is that the first artifact-heavy lesson behaves like a required form more than a coached product-thinking step.

### Decision logic

- If only one change ships quickly, make examples and quality criteria more inline.
- If two changes ship, add earlier diagnostic feedback.
- If three changes ship, improve the post-completion handoff so the student feels the system is building something durable.

### What not to do yet

- Do not spend time on cosmetic restyling without improving judgment support.
- Do not overbuild AI evaluation of student answers in this pass.
- Do not redesign the full shell before fixing the lesson-specific activation issues.

## Priority Plan

### Must

- Surface quality criteria inline for `1.1`
- Bring essential examples closer to the fields
- Improve pre-completion and completion-time feedback specificity
- Reword missing-video fallback so the page still feels intentional

### Should

- Reframe the objective rail and next-step language around the decision, not the mechanic
- Change completion flow to a user-owned transition instead of forced redirect

### Could

- Add a side-by-side final comparison view of the three candidate problems
- Add lightweight quality heuristics for vague inputs

## Validation Plan

### Qualitative validation

- Run 5 focused usability sessions on `1.1` only.
- Ask each participant to explain:
  - what this lesson is trying to help them do
  - how they know a candidate problem is strong
  - what happens after they finish

### Behavioral validation

- Track draft-save events, completion attempts, completion failures, and transition into `1.2`.
- Compare average time spent in `1.1` before and after the changes.
- Monitor whether support questions about "what should I write?" decline.

### Success criteria

- More students complete `1.1` on the first serious pass
- More students can articulate why one candidate problem is stronger than another
- The step from `1.1` to `1.2` feels continuous rather than abrupt

## Narrative Summary

`1.1` is carrying first-real-work energy for the course, so it needs to do more than collect text. Right now the lesson has a solid structure, but the experience still behaves like a gated worksheet: three repetitive inputs, examples pushed off to the side, generic validation, and a fast system-led transition onward. That is functional, but it underserves the actual student job here, which is to build product judgment and confidence at the same time.

The recommended path is to make the lesson feel more coached in the moment of writing. Put the quality bar inline, move the examples closer to the work, make feedback diagnostic instead of generic, and make completion feel like a meaningful handoff rather than a quick redirect. If that happens, `1.1` stops being "the first form" and starts acting like the first proof that the course knows how to help a capable builder make better product decisions fast.
